# Chapter 14: Collateral

"Yes," I said.

I'd thought, on the hundredth floor and in the stairwell on Callow Street and on the roof of Harlan Hall, that if I ever said it out loud, I'd need a speech. I'd drafted a few. None of them were any good. In the end it was one word in the middle of a crowded plaza, with a stranger's elbow in my back and the smell of fried dough from the stalls.

Nora didn't move. She didn't cry, and she didn't hit me. She looked at me for a long moment, and then she nodded once, the way you nod at a receipt that comes out exactly as bad as you expected.

"Not here," she said.

She walked to the hotel without me. I followed her at a distance of about ten meters, which felt correct.

---

She didn't talk to me that night. She slept in Owen's room, on the floor, and Owen came to my door at eleven, holding his clipboard against his chest like a hot water bottle, and said, "She's okay. She's not okay. But she's okay," and went back.

I sat on the bed with the ledger on my knees, the real one, the cheap spiral notebook I'd bought at the Market District stalls on Day Four for two Merit, and did the math for Day Thirteen four times. It came out the same every time. I'd known it would. I did it anyway.

On the morning of Day Eleven she came up to the roof, where I'd been since six.

The hotel roof had a broken air-conditioning unit, a plastic chair, and a view of the Spire. She didn't take the chair. She sat on the edge of the unit with her boots dangling and her arms crossed and said, "Go on, then."

So I told her.

Not some of it this time. All of it. The hundredth floor. The Lender's chair. One percent and one and a half. The collateral clause I'd negotiated, my existence for my most valuable possession, thinking I'd been clever, thinking I'd be putting up a coat. The four words I'd read half a second too late. *To be appraised at regression.* The window in the stairwell.

"The first thing I saw after I woke up," I said, "was your texts. The second thing was the loan account. *Collateral on file: Nora Marsh.* I was standing on the stairs with one shoe untied."

"Day Zero," she said.

"Day Zero."

"So in the flood." Her voice didn't change. "In the dorm. When you jumped on the Librarian. On the roof with the seventy-eight Merit."

"Yes."

"Every single time you did math in front of me, I was one of the numbers."

"Yes."

She turned and looked at the Spire for a while. The wind on the roof was cold for the first time since the world ended. Somebody on the street below was hammering a sign onto a stall.

"You know what I keep thinking about?" she said. "Not that you put me up. You didn't put me up. He took the thing you loved most because you were dying and you didn't read four words. That's a bad contract. You were bleeding. Fine." She uncrossed her arms. "What I keep thinking about is the roof. Harlan Hall. I asked you what happens if you don't pay, and you said *something I can't afford to lose.* And I sat there next to you and believed you meant yourself."

"I know."

"You let me do the math on everything except the one number that was me." Now her voice did something. She got it back. "That's what I'm angry about. You told me about the bakery. You told me about the cinnamon things. You didn't tell me about this."

"I didn't know how."

"Nobody knows how. You say it anyway. That's how." She held out her hand. "Give me the ledger."

I gave it to her. The notebook, not the burned page. She flipped through it, all my columns, the cores and the salve and the jars and the shares, the 692 and the 13 and the 1,832, until she reached the last page with writing on it. *Day 10. Kiln, first clear. 720 + 63. Bal. 2,615.*

She took the pen out of the spiral and drew a line down the middle of the next blank page. On the left she wrote *E.* On the right she wrote *N.*

"New rule," she said. "From now on, I see every number. Every installment. Every statement he sends. Every time you sell something to him, I want to know what it was and what he paid. We keep this together. Both columns."

"That's not—"

"That's the condition." She held the pen out to me. "Or I go find a way to pay him myself, and you know I'll be bad at it, and you'll have to watch."

I took the pen. I wrote *2,615* on the left. Then I stopped, because I didn't know what went on the right.

She took the pen back and wrote, on her side, in her round handwriting that hadn't changed since middle school: *Collateral, Loan 7. Collateral, Loan 6. Lv. 13. Not for sale.*

"There," she said. "Now it's on the books."

---

On Day Twelve, at nine in the morning, Damon Kreel paid.

He didn't come. I'd half expected him to, in the old armor he hadn't sold yet, smiling, making it into a speech about honor between creditors. He didn't send anyone either. The Merit just arrived, the way the Exchange's transfers do, with a small gold chime that only I could hear.

**[Transfer received: 180 Merit.]**
**[Sender: Damon Kreel.]**
**[Memo: "Day 12."]**
**[Balance: 2,795 Merit]**

Two words of memo. Not even a sentence.

"He paid on time," Nora said. She was sitting across from me at the breakfast table in the hotel lobby, which wasn't a breakfast table, just a desk with the computer taken off it, and she'd been watching my face. "Why do you look like that?"

"Because he paid on time."

"That's good."

"The old Damon would've been late on purpose. He'd want me to come to him." I turned my coffee cup around on the desk. It was instant, made with water from the hotel kettle. "This one paid at nine sharp and didn't say anything. That means he's got the money from somewhere, and he doesn't want to talk to me about where."

Nora wrote *180 — D.K.* in the left column. She hesitated, then added a small question mark beside it.

---

Day Thirteen came up gray.

At eleven fifty, we were in the plaza. All five of us. I hadn't asked anyone to come. They came anyway. Jonah sat on the edge of the dry fountain with his bow across his knees. Owen stood with his clipboard. Bram leaned against the Spire wall like a parked truck. Nora stood next to me, close enough that our sleeves touched.

**[Second Installment: 2,000 Merit. Due: 00:09:41]**
**[Balance: 2,795 Merit]**

"You can just pay it," Jonah said. "You don't have to wait till noon."

"I know."

"So why are you waiting?"

"Because he comes when it's due," I said. "And I want to see him come."

At 11:58 the plaza went quiet around me.

Not silent. The noise just stepped back, the way it had on the roof of Harlan Hall. A pigeon on the fountain froze with its head half turned. Jonah's hand stopped halfway to his face. Owen's pen sat on the clipboard mid-letter.

The Lender was sitting in his chair three meters away, on the plaza stones, with his ledger open on his knee.

"Mr. Marsh," he said. "Two minutes early. You're developing habits."

"I'd like to pay the second installment."

"I'd like to receive it." He turned a page. "Two thousand, from a balance of two thousand seven hundred ninety-five. You'll be left with seven hundred ninety-five. Shall I?"

"Go ahead."

**[Second Installment: 2,000 Merit — PAID]**
**[Balance: 795 Merit]**

I'd expected to feel something big. I felt something small. The way you feel when you put down a heavy bag you've carried for ten blocks: not relief, just your arms remembering they're arms.

Then the Lender did something he'd never done before.

He turned his head.

Not toward me. The smooth dark oval where a face should have been turned, slowly, the way a man turns to look at something on a shelf, until it was pointed at Nora.

She was frozen like everyone else, mid-breath, one hand on my sleeve. She couldn't see him. She was looking at the empty air beside me where the chair was, with a small frown, as if she'd caught a draft.

The Lender looked at her for a long time.

"Don't," I said.

"I'm only looking, Mr. Marsh." His voice was as pleasant as ever. "Looking is free. Level thirteen. A first clear. A great many people in this city now know her name." He tilted his head, a few degrees. "She's been reappraised by the market. Not by me. I'd never be so crude."

"She's not on the market."

"Everything is on a market, Mr. Marsh. You taught me that on the hundredth floor." He closed his ledger. "Your statement."

**[LOAN ACCOUNT — STATEMENT]**
**[Second Installment: Paid.]**
**[Outstanding Principal: 11,993 Merit (interest accrued at 1.5% daily).]**
**[Third Installment: 3,500 Merit. Due: Day 23, 12:00.]**

I did the math, because I always do the math, and because I wanted to see if he'd cheated. Twelve thousand fifty-seven on Day Three. Ten days at one and a half percent, compounding. One point zero one five to the tenth is about one point one six. Twelve thousand fifty-seven times that is thirteen thousand nine hundred ninety-three, give or take a few tenths. Minus two thousand.

"Eleven thousand nine hundred ninety-three," I said.

"You never trust me."

"I trust you exactly. That's the problem."

He stood up, picked up the chair, and was gone.

The pigeon finished turning its head. Owen's pen finished its letter. Nora's hand tightened on my sleeve, and she shivered, all at once, hard, like someone who'd walked through a cold room.

"Something was here," she said. "Right here. It's cold on this side."

"He came to collect."

"He was *looking* at me." She wasn't asking. She'd felt it. "Wasn't he."

I thought about the ledger, the two columns. *Every number.*

"Yes," I said. "For the first time."

She was quiet. Then she held out her hand for the notebook, and I gave it to her, and she wrote it down on her side. *Day 13, 12:00. Paid 2,000. He looked.* And then the statement, the principal and the new due date, in the left column, in her round handwriting, so it would be in both of our hands.

"Three thousand five hundred," she said. "Ten days."

"Seven ninety-five in the account. Two thousand seven hundred five to find."

"Okay," she said, the same way she'd said it on the library steps. "Then let's go get it."

---

Silas Rook found us in the late afternoon.

He'd spent most of the last six days at the edge of the plaza, in a doorway near the Ashgrave notice board, watching the gray-corded people from his guild walk past him without seeing him. He looked worse each day. His Duelist's coat had a stain on the left cuff he hadn't bothered to wash out.

He stood in front of me for a moment with his hands in his pockets.

"Congratulations," he said. He didn't smile. "Second installment."

"You paid yours?" I asked.

"I paid my first one on Day Seven. With twenty-two people." He looked across the plaza, to where two of them were standing guard outside the Ashgrave office with their empty faces. "I suppose that counts as paid."

"Damon paid his interest yesterday morning," I said. "On the minute. Do you know where he got it?"

He looked at me, briefly, the way you look at a card before deciding whether to play it.

"Ashgrave employs people," he said. "Not only the ones it seizes."

He didn't say anything else. He nodded once to Bram, who didn't nod back, and went back to his doorway.

---

The practice started by accident.

Bram and Nora were standing in the open middle of the plaza after the Lender left, and Jonah, bored, threw a pebble at Nora's head. She blocked it with the flat of her axe. He threw two. Bram stepped in and took the second on his shield without looking.

"Again," Bram said.

By evening, there was a small crowd. Jonah and four kids from the stalls were throwing everything they could find, pebbles, plastic bottles, a rotten apple. Bram stood planted with his tower shield. Nora stood at his left shoulder, half a step back, axe up, taking whatever came round the edge of the shield.

"No," Bram said, for the tenth time. "You keep stepping out. Stay on my shoulder."

"It's coming at you!"

"Then let it come at me. That's my job." He blocked a bottle. "One shield, everything hits you. Two, they have to choose. Monsters are bad at choosing. They hesitate. The hesitation is the whole point."

Nora stepped out again, caught an apple on her axe, and swore.

"Shoulder," Bram said.

She stepped back onto his shoulder. The next bottle came, and she didn't move, and Bram took it, and the one after came round the left side and she knocked it down, and neither of them moved a centimeter from the line.

"There," Bram said. "That's two."

Nora looked across the plaza at me. I was sitting on the fountain with the ledger, and I hadn't written anything for twenty minutes.

I knew what she was doing. I didn't say anything. I wrote *Shields: 2* at the bottom of her column, and she saw me do it, and turned back to the bottles.

---

The notice went up at dusk.

The Ashgrave board was a strip of the public message wall that had turned gray on Day Seven, as if someone had painted over it. Every day or two, a new notice appeared there, in black letters on gray. Most of them were lists of names.

This one wasn't a list. People were already standing in front of it when we walked past.

**[ASHGRAVE RECOVERIES]**
**[Notice of Engagement — Field Agent Agreement Executed.]**
**[Agent: Damon Kreel.]**
**[Region: Fourteen. Effective: Day 13.]**

I read it twice. On the third time I looked past it.

Vesper Kaine was standing at the corner of the board, in her gray coat, her hands folded in front of her the way a clerk folds them at a counter. And beside her, half a step behind, the way a junior stands beside a senior, was Damon.

He wasn't wearing his armor. He was wearing a plain dark shirt. The gray armband with the white V, the one he'd made thirty-one people wear, was gone.

Around his left wrist was a gray cord.

The same cord as the twenty-two. Tied in the same knot. But his eyes weren't empty. His eyes were exactly the same as they'd been on the hundredth floor, and they found me across the plaza, and he smiled.

I appraised the cord. I didn't even think about it.

**[Ashgrave Binding (Agent) — Status: Worn voluntarily.]**

Nora's hand closed on my arm.

"He tied it on himself," I said.
