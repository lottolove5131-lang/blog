# Chapter 3: The Drowned Campus

There's a rule every Appraiser learns in their first month, usually the hard way: never appraise something that can hear you think.

I'd learned it in Year One, from a wraith in a subway tunnel that went from ignoring me to chasing me the instant I read its price. Most monsters don't notice. The smart ones feel it, like someone reaching into their pocket.

The Hollow Matron had felt it.

The current came back toward me through the black water, slow and steady, pushing against my waist. I could see it now, barely, in the red flicker of the emergency light down the hall. A long pale shape under the surface, as wide as a car and much longer, curving along the corridor. Every few seconds, a ridge of something like fins broke the water and slid back under.

I didn't run. You can't run in waist-deep water. You can only wade very quickly and die tired.

I appraised it again instead. There's no point being careful after the first mistake.

**[Appraise]**

**[Hollow Matron — Zone Beast (Lv. 19)]**
**[Market Value: 2,000 Merit | True Value: 2,600 Merit]**
**[Weakness: None available at current level.]**
**[Behavior: Drawn to the object of highest value within 30 meters.]**

Level nineteen. I was level one. If it touched me, I wouldn't even be a fight. I'd be a stain.

But I read the fourth line twice, because it was new. The old Appraise had never told me what a monster *wanted*. Ledger-Bound did, and it had put it in the same flat voice it used for everything else, as if this were just another price.

*Drawn to the object of highest value.*

I wasn't that object. I'd been appraised at three Merit on the day I died, and I was worth less than that now. The letter opener was nothing. The healing draught was five.

My left hand was already inside my coat, closing around the Gilded Compass.

**[Gilded Compass — Market Value: 30 Merit | True Value: 220 Merit]**

The most valuable thing within thirty meters, by a mile. The reason it had turned around. Not me. It had never been me.

It was also the other half of my first payment. At thirty-five percent, the Lender would give me seventy-seven Merit for it. Maybe more, once I figured out what it did.

The Matron's fin broke the water three meters away.

I threw the compass as hard as I could down the corridor, into the dark, away from the red light. It hit the water with a small, stupid splash.

The current swung away from me like a door slamming. The water sucked back from my waist so hard I nearly fell. Something vast and pale went past me in the dark, close enough that the wake slapped me in the chest, and then it was gone down the corridor after the compass, and I was alone, soaked, shaking, and seventy-seven Merit poorer.

**[First Installment: 692 Merit. Due: 69:58:40]**
**[Balance: 0 Merit]**

Zero. I'd spent the whole balance on the transfer. I'd known that. Seeing it written down was different.

"Worth it," I told the dark, and started wading toward the stairs.

---

The transfer notice was still hanging at the edge of my vision, and on the way up I finally read the small text under it.

**[Transfer destination: Nearest valid point to Borrower's Collateral.]**

So the Lender had dropped me as close to Nora as the rules allowed. He'd also dropped me directly into the part of the building I'd told her never to go near, next to the one monster that could kill everyone in it.

I decided not to think about whether that was an accident.

The stairwell sign said HARLAN HALL, and underneath that someone had taped up a flyer for a Thursday night trivia event. I climbed. The water came up to the second step of the ground floor landing and stopped there, flat and black, lapping gently at the concrete.

The ground floor was a lobby with a front desk and a wall of mailboxes and a set of glass doors. Through them I could see the campus.

It had been a pretty campus once. I remembered visiting for Nora's orientation, the brick buildings and the old oak trees and the long lawn that ran down to the river. The lawn was gone. The whole quad was a lake of flat dark water, and the trees stood up out of it like the masts of sunken ships. The sky above was not the gold of Callow Street. It was a sick, pale green, like the light before a storm, and it didn't move.

A few bodies floated face down near the doors.

I made myself look at them, one at a time, until I was sure none of them were her. Then I went up.

---

**[ZONE RULE: The Drowned Campus]**
**[The water will rise by one floor every 12 hours.]**
**[The flood will recede if the Drowned Librarian is slain.]**
**[Time until next rise: 09:47:13]**

The notice popped up on the second-floor landing, and I stood there in the stairwell and read it until I understood it.

Harlan Hall had four floors and a roof. Basement now. Ground floor in ten hours. Then second, third, fourth. The roof would go under a few hours before the tutorial ended.

Sometime around the morning of Day Three.

I'd spent seven years wondering what exactly had killed my sister. I'd imagined a hundred monsters. It had never occurred to me that it might have just been the water. That she'd have climbed as high as she could, with everyone else, and waited on a roof for someone to come, and nobody had.

I climbed faster.

---

The fourth floor hallway was dry and quiet. Doors on both sides, most of them open, dorm rooms left in a hurry: unmade beds, a phone charger still plugged in, a poster for a band I'd never heard of. At the end of the hall was a study lounge with a heavy wooden door. It was shut. Someone had pushed a vending machine halfway across it from the inside, and there were fresh scratch marks on the outside of the door, deep ones, at about waist height.

I could hear voices behind it. Low, scared, arguing.

I stood there for a second, getting my breath back.

Then I knocked. Two quick, a pause, one slow.

It's a stupid thing. When Nora was six and I was eleven, after Dad left, she used to get scared of the dark and come knock on my door in the middle of the night. She'd do it in a pattern, so I'd know it was her and not a monster. Two quick, a pause, one slow. I hadn't heard it since she was twelve.

The voices behind the door stopped.

Then the vending machine scraped back across the floor, and the door opened about a foot, and my sister looked out at me over the head of a red fire axe.

She was taller than I remembered. That was the first thing, and it made no sense, since I'd last seen her at twenty and she was twenty now. Maybe I'd just gotten used to remembering her smaller. Her hair was tied back badly, she had someone else's blood on the sleeve of her hoodie, and her face was the exact shape of my mother's when she was angry.

"*Eli?*"

"Hi."

She stared at me. Then she put the axe down against the wall, stepped out into the hallway, and punched me in the shoulder as hard as she could.

"You said *lock the door*!"

"You did lock the door."

"You didn't say what to do when something tried to *eat* the door!" She hit me again, less hard. Her voice was cracking. "There were four of us at first. Then people started coming up the stairs, screaming, and I couldn't just, I couldn't leave them out there, so I let them in, and then there were twelve, and then that *thing* came up the hall, and I didn't know what to do, and you weren't answering your phone because the phones don't *work*, Eli, and I thought, I thought—"

I hugged her.

I think I held on too long. I know I did, because after a while she stopped talking and stood very still, and then, carefully, she hugged me back, the way you'd hug someone you weren't sure was okay.

"You're soaking wet," she said into my coat.

"I came up through the basement."

"You told me not to go to the basement."

"I know. I'm a hypocrite." I let go of her and stepped back before I could embarrass myself any further. Behind her, through the gap in the door, eleven faces were watching us. Students. Scared, some hurt, one with a clipboard clutched to his chest like a shield. "How did you get here?" I asked, mostly to say something.

"How did *I* get here? I live here. How did *you* get here? The map thing says the zones are locked. You live on the other side of the city."

"I found a way around."

"What way?"

"An expensive one."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her decide not to push. Nora had always been smarter than me about when to push.

"You knew," she said quietly. "On the phone. You knew exactly what was going to happen."

"Some of it."

"How?"

"I'll tell you everything," I said, "once we're out of here. I promise. Right now I need you to trust me for about three more days."

"That's a lot of trusting."

"I'm good for it."

She snorted. It was the most familiar sound in the world. "You've literally never been good for anything. You still owe me forty dollars from Christmas."

"I'll add it to my list."

---

They'd barricaded themselves well, for kids who'd been living normal lives two hours ago. The vending machine against the door, the couches stacked behind it, the water from the bathroom sinks collected in trash cans in case the pipes went. The one with the clipboard was an RA, and he'd written down everyone's name and class on it, which was the most useful thing anybody in the room had done so far. I read it over his shoulder while Nora explained me to the others.

Most of them had picked the way people always picked on Day Zero. Two Warriors. A Ranger with no bow. Three Clerics, which was lucky. A couple of Artisans.

Nora's line on the list said *Warden.*

"It was the one with the shield," she told me when I asked. She was sitting on the arm of a couch, taping up a cut on the back of her hand. "The description said something about protecting people. It seemed like a good idea at the time."

"It was a good idea."

"Was it? It doesn't do anything. I don't have a shield. I have a fire axe and a skill called *Hold the Line* that makes things want to attack me more. How is that good?"

In the old life, the best Wardens in the world could stand in front of a Spire boss for ten minutes while their raid took it apart. There were only about two hundred of them. Almost none had survived their tutorials, because the class did exactly what she said it did. It made things attack you more. Wardens were only good when someone else was there to do the killing.

"It's good," I said. "It just needs a partner."

She looked at me sideways. "You picked Appraiser, didn't you."

"How did you know?"

"Because it's the one that doesn't make you fight, and you're you." She finished taping her hand. "No offense."

"None taken."

Across the lounge, through the big window, the green sky hadn't changed. Out past the flooded quad, on the far side of the water, a low brick building with tall arched windows stood alone, lit from the inside with a faint, cold light. There was a sign over the doors, too far away to read. I didn't need to read it.

The library.

**[Time until next rise: 09:12:55]**

"That's where we're going," I said.

Nora followed my eyes. "That's across the lake."

"Yes."

"The lake with the thing in it."

"There are several things in it," I said. "But yes."

---

Later, when the others were asleep, or pretending to be, Nora and I sat on the floor by the window with our backs to the wall. The emergency lights in the hallway had gone out an hour ago. The only light in the room was the green sky and the cold glow from the library across the water.

"It's Mom's birthday tomorrow," she said.

"I know."

"I bought flowers. They're in my room. Stupid, right? I kept thinking about them the whole time that thing was scratching at the door. Like, *well, I guess I'm not going to the cemetery now.*" She pulled her knees up. "Is that weird?"

"No."

"You'd tell me if I was being weird."

"I'd tell you if you were being weird."

She was quiet for a while. Then she said, "You look older."

I didn't answer that.

"Not, like, *old*. Just. Around the eyes." She was looking at me, and I kept looking at the window. "You walked in here like you've done this before."

"Go to sleep, Nora."

"Fine." She slid down the wall until her head was resting against my shoulder, like she used to do in the back seat of the car on long drives, when she was little and I was the one in charge of keeping her from kicking the seats. "Wake me up if something tries to eat the door."

"I will."

She was asleep in about two minutes. She always could do that, anywhere. I used to hate her for it.

I sat there with her weight against my shoulder and listened to her breathe, and after a long time, I did the thing I'd been avoiding since the stairwell on Callow Street.

I hadn't appraised her. Not in the hallway, not on the couch, not once. I told myself it was because it didn't matter. That I already knew what I'd see: some number, big or small, and whatever it was, it would feel like a lie. My sister wasn't a price.

That was part of it. The other part was that I was afraid.

But the Lender had her on file. He'd put her in his ledger as my most valuable possession, and I didn't know what that meant. What it cost. What else it said. A good Appraiser doesn't sign a contract without reading every line, and I had already signed.

So I read it.

**[Appraise]**

**[Nora Marsh — Human — Warden (Lv. 2)]**
**[Market Value: — Not for sale.]**
**[True Value: — Not for sale.]**
**[Status: Pledged as Collateral.]**
**[Lienholder (Current): Private Lender. Account: Eli Marsh.]**

I let out a breath. That was what I'd expected. It made me sick, but I'd expected it.

The window didn't close. There was one more line underneath, in the same paper color, but the letters were darker, and older, like ink that had soaked into the page a long time ago.

**[Lienholder (Prior): E█i M█rsh.]**
**[Recorded: Year 9, Day 3.]**
**[Priority: Senior to all later claims.]**

I read it three times.

Someone had already borrowed against my sister. Someone whose name had my letters in it, the ones the ledger hadn't scratched out. Someone whose claim came first.

And they'd signed it in Year Nine.

I died in Year Seven.
