# Chapter 12: Ledger Wall

"One rule," I said.

It was 05:40 on Day Nine, and the Spire plaza was gray and cold and mostly empty. The stool renters hadn't come out yet. The black door breathed heat across the stones in slow, regular waves, and the five of us stood just outside the reach of it, with our packs on and our breath smoking.

"Nobody uses Hold the Line."

I said it to the group. Everybody knew who I meant.

Nora's jaw went tight. She had her axe over one shoulder and a strip of Emberleaf-stained cloth tied around her forehead to keep the sweat out of her eyes, which Bram had shown her the day before. She looked at me for a long moment.

"There's one person here who has Hold the Line," she said.

"Then it's an easy rule to remember."

"Eli."

"Bram holds the front. That's his job. You're second row. You hit whatever Bram stops. Jonah shoots, Owen heals, I count." I kept my voice flat. "If something gets past Bram, you step back. You don't step up. You don't call anything to you. Not once."

"And if something's about to kill Owen?"

"Then I'll be wrong, and you can tell me so at the funeral."

Owen made a face. "Thanks."

Nora opened her mouth. I watched her think about it. I watched her think about the library door, and the letters in the waterline, and the line on the plaza two days ago, and whatever she'd decided after she'd looked at me for so long in the hotel room, reading.

"Fine," she said.

She didn't say anything else. She walked to the door and stood behind Bram, half a step to his left, where I'd told her to stand, with her knuckles white on the axe handle. It was the first time in either of my lives I'd seen her swallow something she wanted to say. I didn't like how it looked on her.

Bram turned his head and looked down at her. "Second row's still a row," he said.

"I know."

"If the first row goes, the second row becomes the first. So the second row doesn't get to go. That's harder."

She didn't answer. She shifted her grip on the axe, and some of the white went out of her knuckles.

---

The Kiln was a clock, and by the third cycle we were inside it.

Sprint. Alcove. Count. Sprint.

The corridor went on the way it had on Day Six, thirty meters wide, the stone walls glowing faintly at the seams, the sigils set into both sides at chest height. Every six hundred seconds, the first sigil lit, and then the next, five seconds apart, and I called them out loud from the back of whatever alcove we'd reached. One. Two. Three. By the sixth, everyone was in, Bram last, dragging his shield in behind him. Then the white fire, five seconds of it. Then the long cool silence, and the next sprint.

Bram's Ember Ward was twenty seconds of fifty-five percent less fire, on a five-minute cooldown. A cycle was ten minutes. I'd done that math so many times on the back of the hotel menu that it came out on its own.

"Two wards per cycle, if we need them," I said, the first time we stopped. "If we're caught in the open, Bram pops it on the fourth sigil, not the first. Fourth sigil is fifteen seconds into the warning. Fifteen left, plus five of fire, is twenty. Exactly one ward. Then it's five minutes before he's got it again, and nine and a half before the next fire. We never get caught twice without it."

"You said that last night," Jonah said.

"I'm saying it again."

"You said it four times last night."

"Then this is five."

We passed the fourth alcove, where we'd killed the Cinder Bailiff, and the fifth. There were constructs in the side hatches between six and seven, the small kind no taller than Owen, and they came out in pairs with their forearm vents coughing. Bram took them on the shield. Nora stepped out from behind his left shoulder and put the axe through their grates, the way she had on Day Six, and stepped back. Every time. She never moved past the line of his shield.

Jonah shot one through the vent before it could cough. Owen kept his riot shield up at our backs and said nothing.

We didn't stop to strip any of them. Salvage was weight, and weight was seconds.

At the seventh alcove, we found where the Cinder Pact had stopped.

Somebody had dropped a canteen. There was a strip of red cloth caught on a crack in the alcove wall, scorched brown on one edge, and on the floor, a porter's frame, empty, with one strap torn off. Bram stood over the frame for a second. Then he picked it up and leaned it carefully against the back of the alcove, upright, the way you'd leave something you meant to come back for.

Nobody said anything.

Jonah picked up the canteen and shook it. Half full. He set it down next to the frame. "In case he comes back," he said. We all knew he wouldn't.

---

Eight was farther than it should have been. The corridor bent left, then right, and the heat changed. It was drier here. It got into your eyes. The sigils were set closer together, and their glow was redder.

"Nine's after the next bend," Bram said. He didn't know that. He said it like something he'd decided to believe.

He was right. The ninth alcove was deeper than the others, almost a little room cut into the left wall, and it was the first one where I couldn't feel the heat at all once I was all the way inside. The back wall was in shadow. Owen held up his clipboard flashlight, the small one he'd taken from the RA desk on Day One.

The light hit the stone.

"Oh," said Owen.

The back wall had been scorched in lines. Long, straight, horizontal lines, evenly spaced, the color of dried rust, running from one side of the alcove to the other. Two vertical lines crossed them near the left edge. It looked exactly like a page from a ledger. It looked exactly like the page I'd been carrying inside my coat since the library, with columns ruled in faded red ink, except this one was the size of a door and burned into a wall.

Near the top left corner, someone had made marks. Six of them. Straight vertical strokes, close together, like a tally kept by someone without a pencil.

And under the marks, in the space between two ruled lines, there was writing.

It had been scratched into the soot with something sharp. The letters were uneven and cramped. They leaned hard to the right. The lowercase *t*s were crossed too high, like little *f*s.

*DAY THIRTY. I OPEN THE DOOR FROM MY SIDE.*
*BE THE BEARER.*
*— E.*

---

I read it four times.

The fifth time, I appraised it, because I didn't know what else to do with my hands.

**[Appraise]**

**[Scorched Inscription (Kiln, Floor 1, Alcove 9)]**
**[Market Value: 0 Merit | True Value: —]**
**[Recorded: Year 9, Day 3.]**
**[Note: Delivered through a registered lien. Recipient: Account holder, Loan #7.]**

*Recipient: Account holder, Loan #7.*

Me.

Year Nine, Day Three. The same date as the lien on my sister. The same handwriting as the library door. Somebody in a world that didn't exist anymore had sat in the dark on the day they'd recorded that lien, and scratched a message into a wall they couldn't reach, and sent it through the only crack they had.

"*Be the bearer,*" Nora said, behind me. "What does that mean?"

"I don't know."

"You read contracts for a living."

"I know what the word means," I said. "Bearer. Whoever holds the paper. A bearer bond pays whoever's holding it, no name on it." I stared at the letters. "I don't know what paper. I don't know what it means for *me* to be the bearer of anything. I don't own anything he could mean."

"He," said Nora.

I didn't answer that.

"Day Thirty," said Jonah. "That's the day the gray-coat lady said."

"Yes."

"*I open the door from my side,*" Owen read slowly, holding the light steady. "Which door?"

"The one Vesper saw," I said. "The lien. She said it was shaped like a door."

The wall didn't say anything else.

Nora stepped past me. She didn't look at the writing. She looked at the marks in the corner, the six straight strokes, and she lifted her hand and touched the first one with the tip of her finger, and then the next, and the next, moving left to right, like a kid counting on a number line.

"One," she said, under her breath. "Two. Three. Four. Five."

Her finger stopped on the last one.

"Six."

She stood there with her finger on the sixth mark for a long time. I didn't know what she was thinking. I could guess. There had been seven lines on the ledger page. Five *Defaulted, Collateral seized.* One *Collection pending.* One *Active.* Six marks on a wall, scratched by someone who'd counted himself in.

She took her hand down and wiped the soot off on her trousers.

"Okay," she said. Just that.

I opened her window, standing next to her in the alcove. I didn't ask. She didn't stop me.

**[Message pending (0). Last message: Delivered.]**

The line was still there, under the old ink. Only the number had changed.

---

We rested in the ninth alcove for two cycles. It was the coolest place in the Kiln, and nobody wanted to leave it, and nobody wanted to talk about why.

Bram ate two protein bars and fell asleep sitting up with his shield across his knees, the way I imagine walls sleep. Jonah checked every arrow in his quiver and then checked them again. Owen wrote on his clipboard, very small, at the bottom of the page: *Day 9. Alcove 9. Writing. E.* Then he looked at it, and added: *Not ours to touch.*

Nora sat next to me with her back against the ledger wall, so that the six marks were just above her head. She had her knees pulled up. She wasn't looking at me.

"You'd have told me," she said. "If you knew what it meant."

"Yes."

"You'd have told me *eventually.*"

That one, I didn't answer. She nodded, as if I had.

**[Time: Day 9, 21:14]**

At the end of the second rest I stood up and stretched and looked down the corridor. It went on past the bend, and then, a long way off, it stopped going on. At the far end, in the red glow, there was something that wasn't a wall.

"Boss door," said Bram, awake again without seeming to have woken.

---

It took us four more cycles to reach it. The alcoves past nine were shallower, and the constructs came out in threes, and on the second-to-last sprint we got caught in the open, all five of us, with three constructs crawling out between us and the next alcove just as the first sigil lit.

Bram went through the constructs like a door going through a crowd. Two. Three. Jonah shot one, Nora axed another, and we ran. Nora had Owen by the back of his collar.

Four.

"*Bram!*"

**[Bram Okafor used Ember Ward.]**

A haze of dull orange spread from the face of his shield, out and around us, like heat off a road in summer. Five. We were still ten meters short. Six.

The discharge came. White fire, five seconds. Through the ward it felt like standing too close to an oven with the door open. My bandaged arm screamed under the cloth. Then it was over, and we were all still standing, and the ward guttered out a second later, and Bram said, mildly, "Fifty-five percent."

"Fifty-five percent," I agreed, and sat down on the floor of the corridor for a second because my legs had stopped listening.

Nora had not used Hold the Line. Not when the constructs came out in threes. Not when we were caught in the open. Her hands had twitched toward it twice. I'd seen them. Both times, she'd looked at me and put her hands back on the axe.

**[Time: Day 9, 23:41]**

The boss door was the size of the courthouse. It was made of black iron, shaped like the open mouth of a furnace, with a lip of dull bronze around the edge. Above it, in the Exchange's gold:

**[Spire, Floor 1 — Boss Chamber]**
**[Warning: Entry is final until the Boss is slain or all party members are removed.]**

"Removed," said Owen, quietly.

"It means dead," said Nora.

"I know what it means," Owen said. "I'm just saying it out loud."

I looked at the four of them. Bram, with his scorched shield. Jonah, with an Iron Wolf's bow. Owen, with his clipboard and a riot shield that said CAMPUS SECURITY. My sister, with a fire axe from a dormitory hallway and a skill she'd promised not to use.

Two thousand Merit by noon on Day Thirteen. A hundred and sixty-eight short. Twenty-four hundred for the first clear.

"We go in," I said. "And I watch the numbers."

---

The boss chamber was round, built of black brick under a dome stained orange by a thousand old fires, and it was hot in a different way from the corridor. None of the slow pulse of the sigils. A steady, dense heat, like the inside of a closed fist.

The thing at the far end of it was very tall and very still.

Its body was a balance scale. An iron post for a spine, an iron crossbeam for shoulders, and from each end of the beam a chain hung down to a bronze pan the size of a cartwheel. The pans swayed slightly, the way a scale sways when nothing has been put on it yet. And in the middle of its chest, where a heart would be, there was a crucible. A clay pot as wide as a bathtub, glowing white at the bottom, with a heavy iron lid closed over it.

It had no face. Above where its face should have been, in gold:

**[The Assayer — Floor Boss (Lv. 19)]**

I raised my hand to appraise it.

It appraised us first.

I felt it the way I'd felt Vesper on the plaza. A hand in my pocket. A cold, polite pressure behind my eyes, as if someone were reading the label on the inside of my collar. And then, for the first time in either of my lives, I watched something else do what I do, out loud, where everyone could see it.

Gold numbers appeared in the air.

One above each of us.

**[Bram Okafor — True Value: 2,300 Merit]**
**[Jonah Webb — True Value: 880 Merit]**
**[Owen Park — True Value: 1,240 Merit]**

Jonah looked up at the number over his head and said, "Hey."

Above Nora, the gold didn't settle. Digits flickered there and changed and flickered again, like a slot machine that couldn't decide, and under them, for half a second, I saw the edge of something in old-paper ink before the gold covered it. The Assayer's pans hung still, as if it were thinking. Then they moved on.

Slowly, both pans swung through the air, past Bram, past Owen, past my sister, weighing. They stopped. They tilted.

Toward me.

The last number lit above my head. It was bigger than the others. It was the biggest number I'd ever seen attached to my name in either life.

**[Highest-value object present: Appraiser (Class) — Eli Marsh.]**
**[True Value: 41,000 Merit.]**

The iron lid of the crucible slid sideways like an eyelid opening, and the white light inside it turned to face me.
