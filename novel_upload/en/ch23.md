# Chapter 23: Acceleration

"Boats," said Jonah.

It was eleven minutes after the window had changed. We'd folded up the stall, which we had never once done before noon, and carried the tables into the hotel lobby, and now the five of us were sitting around the one in the middle with the cash box on it like it was a body.

"There are boats in the Drowned Campus," Jonah went on. "Rowboats from the boathouse. The river channel goes east out of Zone Nine, past the old water plant. Two days on the water and you're out of the city."

"Zone Four's closer by road," said Bram. "I know the road. I can carry her if she gets tired."

"I'm not going to get tired," said Nora.

"I know a man in Four with a cellar," Bram went on. "Cinder Pact used it for loot. Nobody goes down there."

I already had the map open in my head, the way I'd had the Kiln open in it on Day Nine. River, east, two days. Road, north, a day and a half. Cellar. My heart was going too fast and my brain was doing what it always did when my heart went too fast, which was pricing exits.

"Ashgrave collects at noon on Twenty-Eight," I said. "If we leave tonight, that's two and a half days of distance. If she's past the city limit by then—"

"She'll still be collected," said Silas Rook.

None of us had heard him come in. He was standing by the lobby doors with the light behind him, in the same coat he'd worn on Day Seven, when he sat down next to me on the plaza and told me he knew the sixth one. He looked older than he had then. Most of us did.

"You don't know that," said Nora.

"I do, actually." He walked over and didn't sit. "In Year Nine I was Ashgrave's field agent. You know that by now. You've all looked at me the way you look at somebody who's been explained to you." He put his hands in his coat pockets. "In Year Eight, a man defaulted on a guild loan, and he took a boat. Ninety kilometers down the coast, to a fishing town that wasn't on any map the Exchange cared about. I didn't follow him. I didn't have to."

"What did you do?" said Owen.

"I opened his file." Silas made a small motion with his hand, like turning a page. "And he was standing in front of me. Wet to the knees. He'd been in the boat a second ago. The cord was already on his wrist." He looked at Nora. "Collateral gets collected wherever it is. The gray cord doesn't come by location. It comes by ledger. Your name's on a page, and the page is in their office."

Nobody said anything. Bram looked at his hands.

"So running costs us two days for nothing," I said.

"Running costs you the two days you've got left with her," said Silas. "Those are the most expensive thing you own."

---

I went to the board after that. I didn't know what else to do.

The Public Exchange Board stood in the middle of the courthouse plaza the way it had since Day Four, ten meters of glass full of gold text, scrolling prices and notices and the names of people who'd died in the Spire. Most people only ever used the top third. Listings. Bids. Regional notices.

Below that was the rest of it. The Exchange had a rule for everything, because it was an Exchange, and every rule was posted, because a rule nobody could read wasn't a rule, it was a trap. Last time around, it had taken the world about two years to start reading the bottom of the board. By then most of the people it would have helped were dead.

Owen came with me. He brought his clipboard and a bag of the hard bread from the stall by the fountain and didn't ask what we were looking for, which was good, because I didn't know.

"Start with what they did," he said. "They didn't just say *Day Twenty-Eight*. They used a word."

"Acceleration."

"Then look up the word."

I found it at a quarter to four in the afternoon, in the part of the board that dealt with sold debt. It was eleven lines long. I read it out loud so Owen could copy it.

**[CREDIT RULES — Collateralized Paper, §14: Acceleration of Collection]**
**[A Holder may advance the date of collection where the Collateral's value is impaired.]**
**[Impairment shall be established by the appraisal of the Holder's Appraiser of record.]**
**[Upon filing, the amended schedule takes effect immediately.]**

"Holder's appraiser of record," Owen said, writing. "Who's that?"

"Who do you think?"

He didn't answer. He knew. Gray coat, short pale hair, a face like somebody who was very good at cards.

"So they get to say she's damaged," Owen said, "and the person who says it works for them, and it takes effect right away." He tapped the pen against the board's frame. "That's not a rule. That's a vending machine."

"Every rule has a back door." I kept scrolling. "Somebody wrote these. Somebody with money wanted to be able to argue."

It took another hour and a half. The sun went down behind the courthouse and the board got brighter against the dark. Owen ate half the bread. I ate none of it. Then, at the bottom of §14, in the smallest type on the whole board, there was a reference I'd skipped three times because it looked like a page number.

*See §22.*

**[CREDIT RULES — §22: Appraisal Objection]**
**[Any party holding a recorded interest in the same Collateral may contest a Holder's appraisal.]**
**[Hearing: public. Each party's Appraiser appraises the Collateral before the Board.]**
**[Filed instruments relating to the Collateral are sealed until the hearing and unsealed upon it.]**
**[The Public Exchange Board shall rule. Its ruling is final.]**

I read it twice and felt something unlock in my chest.

"Recorded interest in the same collateral," I said. "The Lender's lien on Nora. Loan Seven. My account."

"So you can object."

"I can object." I was already reading the next line. Then I stopped.

**[Condition of filing: Should the Objection fail, the Objector's Appraisal skill is forfeited to the Holder.]**

Owen read it over my shoulder. He didn't say anything. He wrote it down, very neatly, and then put his pen behind his ear, which was what he did when he didn't want to be the one who said a thing first.

"Forfeited to the Holder," I said. "Not lost. Forfeited to them. If I lose, Ashgrave gets my eyes."

"Your Ledger-Bound eyes."

"The only pair in the city that sees True Value, apart from hers." I closed the window. "They'd have two."

---

There was one more line, further down, under a heading about *parties and standing.* I found it while Owen was packing up. I wished I hadn't.

**[Note: Collateral is not a party.]**

---

I told Nora at midnight. She was on the hotel roof, which was where she went when she needed to be angry somewhere with a lot of air.

I told her everything. The rule, the back door, the hearing, the condition. I showed her Owen's copy. I'd promised. Joint ledger. Every number.

She read it twice. Then she found the last line and read that one four times.

"I'm not a party," she said.

"It's how they write it."

"I'm not a *party* to *myself.*" She was holding the clipboard so hard her knuckles had gone pale. "They get to say I'm broken. You get to say I'm not. I don't get to say anything. I'm the thing on the table."

"Yes."

"And if you lose, they take your eyes."

"Yes."

"And then what? You can't see prices. You can't do the stall. You can't see the lien." She looked at me. "Can you still do the door? On Thirty? If you can't see it?"

I'd done that math in the stairwell on the way up. "The conditions are name, account, and presence. Nothing about seeing. I think I could still do it." I paused. "If there's still a Day Thirty for you. Which there won't be, if I don't file. Because you'll be on a cord on Twenty-Eight."

She put the clipboard down on the gravel. She looked out at the city, which was dark except for the board, glowing gold at the center of everything like a stove.

"You were going to do it anyway," she said. "Even if I said no."

"I was going to ask you first."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," I said. "But it's what I can do."

She was quiet for a long time. Then she held out her hand, and I gave her the pencil, and she opened the ledger on her knee, one-handed, and turned to the next clean line on her side and wrote:

*Day 25. Objection filed. Risk: Eli's Appraise. Approved — N.*

She wrote her initial and then, after a second, wrote mine next to it.

"Joint ledger," she said. "If you lose your eyes, I lose them too. I'm going to make you read everything out loud for the rest of your life."

---

I filed at nine in the morning on Day Twenty-Six. You filed at the board. You put your hand flat on the glass, and the board read your account, and then it read you.

**[APPRAISAL OBJECTION — Filing]**
**[Objector: Eli Marsh. Interest: Lien (Current), Loan 7, Private Lender.]**
**[Respondent: Ashgrave Recoveries. Holder, Loan 6.]**
**[Contested Appraisal: Impairment of Collateral (Nora Marsh), filed Day 25.]**
**[Condition acknowledged: Objector's Appraisal skill at stake.]**
**[Confirm?]**

I'd haggled with a faceless man on the hundredth floor with forty-four minutes left to live. This was easier. It just felt worse.

**[Confirmed.]**

Then one more line came up, and it wasn't anything I hadn't expected, but my stomach went cold anyway.

**[Respondent's Appraiser of record: Vesper Kaine.]**

When I took my hand off the glass, she was standing behind me.

She hadn't been there a second ago. She never was.

"You read to §22," Vesper said. "Most people stop at fourteen."

"You knew it was there."

"I know where everything is, Mr. Marsh. It's my job." She looked past me at the filing window, which was still glowing on the glass. "You've put up your eyes."

"I've put up what I had."

"I know." For a moment, her calm face did something I couldn't read. Then it was gone. "I'll see you on the steps."

She walked away toward the courthouse. I appraised her back, out of habit, out of spite, the way I'd appraised the altar on the hundredth floor.

**[Vesper Kaine — True Value: Pledged (Ashgrave).]**

The same line as always. I don't know what I'd expected.

---

We had a day. Owen spent it on the clipboard.

Not his clipboard. All of them. In eleven days of business, Clipboard House had filled four boards' worth of paper on both sides, and he'd kept every sheet in a cardboard box under the table. He sat in the hotel lobby with the box and a lamp, sorting, while the fifteen took turns bringing him tea.

"What are you looking for?" Jonah asked him.

"Every time anybody asked us about a Warden." Owen didn't look up. "Contracts with Warden clauses. Recruiters asking us to pass on offers to Nora. Prices for Warden gear. If they're going to say she's worth less, I want everything that says what people paid for her."

"People didn't pay for her," said Nora, from the stairs.

"People tried to," said Owen. "That counts. On a board, it's called a bid."

By evening, he had a stack of sheets an inch high with a rubber band around it. By evening, too, people had started coming to the hotel door.

The student from Day Fourteen came first, the one who'd tried to sell a silt leech core for three Merit when it was worth six. He stood in the doorway with his hands in his pockets.

"I heard you've got a hearing," he said. "If you need somebody to say you didn't rob me. You could've bought my core for three. You told me it was six." He shrugged. "I'll say it on the steps."

After him, a recruiter from one of the Zone Four guilds who'd brought us a contract on Day Twenty-Three. Then a woman who'd bought a shield from Maya. Then two of the fifteen, who just wanted to know what time to be there.

Owen wrote all their names down.

I sat at the other end of the lobby with the ledger, on my side of Nora's line, and tried to build the case the way I'd build a trade, from the bottom up.

The problem was the top. Ashgrave had bought Loan Six from the Lender. Somewhere there was a piece of paper that said what they'd bought and on what terms, and §22 said it was filed and sealed, and that it would be unsealed at the hearing. Not before.

"So you go in blind," said Jonah, reading over my shoulder.

"I go in with Owen's box. And I read their contract for the first time with the whole city watching."

"That's insane."

"It's how I read Damon's," I said. "Day Six. Same steps. I didn't know what was in Clause Two until a Ranger held hers out."

He thought about it. "You got lucky."

"I got fine print," I said. "Fine print's never luck. Somebody always put it there on purpose. You just have to be the one who reads it."

Just before six I made myself write the numbers down anyway, the ones I had. Nora's level: seventeen. Floors cleared: two. My balance: a hundred and some, which bought nothing that mattered. The stake: one skill, the only thing I'd ever been good at.

---

At six in the evening, the board chimed.

It wasn't the long tone for a regional notice, or the short double tone for a sale over a thousand Merit. It was a sound I'd heard maybe twice in seven years, last time around. Low. Like a gavel on wood.

Every head in the courthouse plaza turned toward it. People came out of the stalls. A man selling water put down his cups.

**[PUBLIC NOTICE — APPRAISAL OBJECTION]**
**[Eli Marsh v. Ashgrave Recoveries.]**
**[Subject: Collateral (Nora Marsh), Loan 6.]**
**[Hearing: Day 27, 12:00. The Old Courthouse Steps.]**
**[Stake: Objector's Appraisal skill.]**

A murmur went through the crowd, starting at the front and moving back, the way a wave goes through water. People turned to look at the hotel doors. At me. At Nora, behind me on the stairs.

Somebody near the fountain laughed, not kindly, and said, "He's betting his class."

Someone else said, "On her?"

Nora came down the last two steps and stood next to me and didn't say anything. Her hand found my sleeve and held it, the way it had on Day Six, on the plaza, when Vesper read my price out loud.

Tomorrow at noon, on the same steps where Damon had made his speech on Day Zero, and where his guild had dropped its armbands on Day Six, I would stand in front of the whole city, and I would tell the Exchange what my sister was worth.

And if the Exchange disagreed, I'd never see a price again.
