# Chapter 9: The Kiln

Jonah and Owen found us at the Spire plaza just after noon, both of them hollow-eyed from walking up out of the tutorial zones overnight. Jonah still had his bow. Owen still had his shield, and a split lip he wouldn't explain, and the particular look of a man who'd heard what happened on the courthouse steps and decided whose side of it he wanted to stand on.

"You could have sent a message," Nora said, hugging Owen hard enough to make him wince.

"You could have waited a day," Jonah said to me. "We're going in there with you."

So we were four. That was the whole party, standing in the cold at the foot of a hundred floors of white stone, watching the black door.

The woman in the gray coat was already there. She hadn't been there a moment before. I was sure of it, because I'd been watching the door, and then she was simply standing ten paces to the left of it, the way a price tag is simply there when you pick something up.

"Vesper Kaine," she said, before I could ask her name. "Mr. Marsh, a word before you go in. Professional courtesy."

"You followed us," Nora said.

"I was already here." Vesper's calm face didn't move. "I wanted to see the Kiln's first-week parties with my own eyes. And I wanted to give you something, since you gave me such a pleasant morning today." She looked at me. "The lien on your sister. The prior one. The one with your name on it, dated two years after your death."

My stomach dropped through the plaza stones.

"You can see it too," I said.

"I can see that it's there. I can't see through it. None of us can, it's sealed by the holder." She paused. "But I can see the *shape* of it, Mr. Marsh. And the shape is wrong. A lien is a claim against value. This one isn't shaped like a claim. It's shaped like a *door*. Someone built it to be opened, from the other side, on a specific day." She glanced at Nora, once. "Day Thirty."

"She is not something I am holding," I said. "She is my sister. Why are you telling me this?"

"Because my firm collects on Day Thirty, and I'd rather collect from a man who understands what he's holding." She smiled, very slightly. "And because doors that open from the other side tend to open whether or not you're ready. Watch the walls in there. The Kiln keeps books too."

She walked away toward the message wall, and didn't look back.

---

The door had opened at 05:00. Seven hours later, it was still spilling heat across the plaza.

Nora went first. That had been the argument, all the way across the plaza, me saying the Warden should be in the middle where she could reach anyone, Nora saying the Warden goes first, Jonah saying he'd put an arrow in the first thing that moved, Owen saying nothing and standing next to Nora, which settled it.

The corridor was exactly the way I remembered it from Week Three of the old life, except that remembering it and standing in it were two different things. Thirty meters wide. Stone walls that already glowed faintly at the seams. And on both walls, set into the stone at chest height, the sigils: six of them, three per side, each one a carved circle with a symbol in the middle that hurt to look at directly.

"Alcoves," I said. "Left side, every thirty meters. The heat doesn't reach the back of them."

"How do you know?" Jonah asked.

"Because four hundred people died learning it," I said. "Move."

We made four hops that way, sprint, alcove, count, sprint, and on the fifth, the sigils started to light.

One. Then another, five seconds later. Then another.

"Thirty seconds," I said, watching the third one catch. "They light one at a time. Then—"

All six discharged at once. White fire screamed out of the wall vents for about five seconds, and the corridor became a furnace, and then, nothing. Cool stone. Silence. The sigils went dark one by one.

We watched through the cooldown, more than nine minutes with the stone dark. "Ten minutes," I said when the first sigil lit again. "The last thirty seconds are the warning."

"Why do they all go at once?" Nora asked from the back of the alcove.

I opened my appraisal on the corridor itself, the way I'd appraised the loan on the hundredth floor. It took the full thirty seconds of the next charge to read it.

**[The Kiln, Floor 1, Cycle: 600 seconds.]**
**[Charge: 30s (sigils ignite sequentially, ~5s apart). Discharge: ~5s (all sigils simultaneously). Cooldown: ~565s.]**
**[Source: single furnace-heart. All six sigils draw from one heart. The discharge is the heart venting pressure, simultaneous by design.]**

"One heart," I said. "Six sigils, one heart. The discharge is the heart letting off pressure. That's why it's simultaneous, it's not six traps, it's one animal breathing."

"That's worse," Jonah said.

"It's better," I said. "Animals have habits. Habits can be priced."

---

We were between the fourth and fifth alcove when the man stepped out of the heat shimmer ahead of us.

He was enormous. Two and a half meters, shoulders like a vault door, carrying a shield as tall as I was. His armor was scorched black, and the armband on his left arm had been torn off, I could see the pale strip where it used to be. He stood in the middle of the corridor, in the open, during the cooldown, and watched us come.

"That's far enough," he called. His voice was rough. "Next discharge in forty seconds. You've got one alcove behind you and none ahead. Talk fast."

"Who are you?" Nora said.

"Bram Okafor. Bulwark, eleventh level. Late of the Cinder Pact." He smiled without humor. "Late because the Pact left a porter in a tutorial side room yesterday, and I went back for him, and the Pact doesn't go back for anyone. So they took my armband." He looked at the four of us. "You're the Marsh party. The appraiser who bought Vanguard's charter. I've been waiting for someone going in deep. Everyone else is going in to die fast. You're going in to come back."

"You want to join us," I said.

"I want to hold your front. I'm good at it. It's the only thing I'm good at." He lifted his shield. "Terms: equal shares. I don't do bait anymore."

I appraised him before I answered. It was rude, and everyone does it anyway.

**[Bram Okafor, Bulwark, Lv. 11. Former guild: Cinder Pact (expelled).]**
**[Record: 14 line-holds. 0 retreats. Owes back-pay: 211 Merit (uncollectible).]**
**[True Value: a wall that chose its own ground.]**

"Equal fifths," I said. "You, me, my sister, Jonah, Owen. Five ways."

"Good," Bram said. "I'm done being bait." He picked up his shield and fell in at the front like he'd always been there. "Equal fifths. And if I die in there, you tell the Pact I died standing somewhere I chose."

---

The thing came out of the far end on the next charge, while the sigils were still lighting one by one.

It was four meters tall and built like a furnace with legs. Its chest was a grate, and behind the grate something burned white. Its arms ended in vents. Across its brow, in the Exchange's gold text:

**[Cinder Bailiff, Lv. 14 (Mini-Boss)]**

"Nora, left alcove!" I shouted. "Jonah, left! Bram, front! Owen, rear, watch the back vents!"

The order set itself: Bram at the front with his shield up, Nora and Jonah in the middle, me behind them, Owen at the rear with his shield covering our backs. The Bailiff's head turned toward Bram, and the grate in its chest brightened.

**[Appraise]**

**[Cinder Bailiff, Market Value: 900 Merit | True Value: 2,400 Merit]**
**[Furnace-Heart: vents every 600s with the corridor sigils. During discharge, the furnace-mouth opens to release pressure. Core exposed: ~5 seconds.]**
**[Weakness: the open mouth. Strike window matches the corridor discharge.]**

"Listen!" I shouted over the rising heat. "The sigils light for thirty seconds, then everything discharges for five! The Bailiff opens its mouth during the discharge to vent! That's when its core is exposed! Jonah, you put an arrow through the open mouth during the five seconds! Not before! During!"

"During the fire?!" Jonah yelled.

"From the alcove! The alcoves are safe during discharge! Bram, Nora, you hold it off the alcoves until then!"

The first sigil lit. Then the second.

Bram planted his shield and the Bailiff charged him, and the corridor shook. Bram took the hit the way a wall takes weather, he gave three steps and stopped it, and his shield glowed at the edges.

Third sigil.

The Bailiff swung its arm toward us. Its vent coughed. Bram turned his shield and caught the flame on its face and let it wash past into the stone.

Fourth sigil. Fifth.

A second construct, no taller than Owen, crawled from a maintenance hatch behind us. Its forearm vent coughed. Owen didn't look back. He stepped sideways, planted, and took the flame on the rim of his shield, and it broke around him like water around a rock. Nora struck the smaller construct through its grate before it could fire again. Its empty shell was not worth stopping for.

Sixth sigil. The corridor held its breath.

"Now!" I screamed. "Alcoves!"

We folded into the alcoves, Bram last, dragging his shield in after him, and all six sigils discharged at once, and the corridor became white fire, and through the white fire I saw the Bailiff's chest grate swing open, and the white core burning inside it like a small sun.

Jonah stood up in the mouth of the left alcove, drew, and loosed, straight through the open furnace-mouth, into the core.

The construct folded to the corridor floor. The fire in its chest went out like a closed account.

Silence. Then the corridor inhaled again, and we ran for the next alcove with the Bailiff's heart-core in Jonah's pack and Bram laughing, actually laughing, for the first time since the Cinder Pact took his armband.

The twenty-Merit fire ward had cut the heat by fifty-five percent. The rest had found my left forearm, wrist to elbow. I'd stayed half a step out of the alcove to keep my eyes on the sigils and call the count, and the edge of the discharge had found me. It hurt the way only burns hurt, and I wrapped it in a strip of my shirt and didn't mention it until Nora saw the blood on the cloth.

"You stayed out," she said flatly.

"Someone had to count," I said.

"You're an idiot."

"It's a family trait."

---

We sold the heart-core through the private sale before we left the Spire's shadow. One core, one sale. I checked the window before confirming the transfer. True Value six hundred, twenty percent, one hundred twenty Merit, no questions asked.

**[Kiln-Heart Core, Market Value: 150 Merit | True Value: 600 Merit]**
**[Private Sale: 20% of True Value = 120 Merit.]**

The furnace plating and brass went on the public board: seventy-five gross, five percent fee rounded up to four whole Merit, seventy-one net. That was everything. No system bonus, no levels, no shower of gold. The Spire paid in salvage, and salvage was enough.

One hundred ninety-one Merit. I wrote it in the ledger under the day's column.

"Equal fifths," I said. "A hundred and ninety-one doesn't split even. Thirty-eight each, one left over."

"I'll take thirty-eight," Nora said.

"Thirty-eight," Jonah said.

"Thirty-eight," Owen said.

"Thirty-eight," Bram said.

I took the odd Merit. Thirty-nine for the appraiser, thirty-eight for everyone else. I marked the extra coin beside my name. Next uneven split, someone else would get it.

**[Balance (Eli): 1,793 + 39 = 1,832 Merit]**
**[Shares paid: Nora 38 | Jonah 38 | Owen 38 | Bram 38 Merit.]**

Two thousand minus one thousand eight hundred thirty-two. One hundred sixty-eight.

"One sixty-eight short," I said out loud.

Bram was sitting against the Spire wall, turning his scorched shield over in his hands. "Equal fifths," he said. "You actually did equal fifths."

"I said equal fifths," I said.

"Nobody does equal fifths," Bram said. "The Pact did shares by rank. Tankers got half shares. Disposable, you know. Cheaper to replace than to pay." He looked at me. "You're a strange appraiser, Eli Marsh."

"I'm a bad appraiser," I said. "I keep finding things worth more than their price. It's a terrible habit."

---

Nora found me after, doing the math again in the ledger, the way you press on a bruise.

"Day Twelve," she said. "Damon's interest. A hundred and eighty."

"One thousand eight hundred thirty-two plus one hundred eighty is two thousand twelve," I said. "Twelve over. If he pays."

"If?"

I looked up at her. "He has fifteen people left, a charter whose debt I can call in full any day I like, and nothing to sell. A hundred and eighty Merit was a rounding error to the old Damon." I closed the ledger. "To this one, it might be everything he has."

"So he might not pay."

"He might not be able to." I stood up, and my burned arm throbbed under the cloth. "I'm not waiting six days to find out which one it is. Tomorrow, I go find Damon Kreel, and I remind him what he owes me, and what happens to the fifteen people still wearing his armband if he doesn't."

"That's not arithmetic," Nora said. "That's a threat."

"It's arithmetic with a deadline," I said. "Same thing, out here."

---

The Lender was waiting at the edge of the square when the others went ahead to the room. He leaned against a wall that wasn't there yesterday, or maybe it was, and I just hadn't owed anyone enough to notice it before. He looked the same as ever: patient, pressed, like a man with all the time in the world because he owned most of it.

"One hundred sixty-eight short," he said. "I do admire the arithmetic. Most of my debtors can't count past their own panic."

"You're not my only hobby," I said. "What do you want?"

"To congratulate you," he said. "And to mention something, as a courtesy. Vesper Kaine's firm bought Loan Six because I sold it. I sell paper when the risk stops being worth the return. It's nothing personal. It's just business." He smiled. "But I thought you should know, you're not my only debtor."

The square's noise faded the way it does when someone says the thing you've been avoiding.

"Meaning what?" I said.

"Meaning I have other accounts. Other installments. Other collateral." He straightened his cuffs. "One of them is in this Spire right now. First installment due tomorrow. And when a debtor defaults, Eli, I sell the paper. Quickly. To whoever will pay." He looked toward the Spire's black door. "I'd hate for you to be nearby when the collection comes. Collections are messy. Almost as messy as families."

He walked away, and I stood there with one hundred sixty-eight Merit between me and the installment, twenty-four days between me and Vesper Kaine's firm, and a new question I hadn't owed yesterday:

Who else in this city had borrowed their way back, and what had they put up as collateral?
