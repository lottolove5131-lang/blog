# Chapter 17: Write-Down

"Not here," Vesper said, before I could answer. "Walk with me, Mr. Marsh. Your sister can keep the tarp."

Nora had already let go of her corner. Bram had let go of his. Owen's pen had stopped moving.

"Ten minutes," I told them. "I'll be under the board."

"You'll be under the board," Nora repeated, in the voice she used when she was writing something down in her head. She didn't stop me. That was new, and I noticed it, and so did Vesper.

---

The public board stood at the north end of the plaza, twenty meters of gold listings hanging in the air over the old fountain. At night it was the brightest thing in the city. People slept under it on cold nights because the light came with a little warmth. There were a few of them now, rolled in blankets along the fountain's rim, and Vesper stepped around them without looking down, the way you step around furniture you've known for years.

She stopped where the listings threw the most light and turned her face up to them.

"Spirit Moss, nine Merit," she read. "That's low. A Zone Two Artisan figured out how to grow it in a bathtub. It'll be four by Day Twenty." She looked at me. "You see two numbers when you look up there. So do I. Did you ever wonder why?"

"Ledger-Bound," I said. "It only works with a private account."

"With *his* account." She put her hands in her coat pockets. "Ask me where I'm from."

I appraised her instead. Old habit, and rude, and she'd done it to me first.

**[Vesper Kaine — Appraiser (Lv. 38)]**
**[Origin: Asset #61,207 (Liquidated)]**
**[True Value: Pledged (Ashgrave Recoveries).]**

"Sixty-one thousand two hundred and seven," I said.

"A small blue world with two moons and very good coffee. We had about the same number of people you do. The Exchange bought us, and onboarded us, and opened a Spire in the middle of my city, and seven years later it reviewed the account." She said it the way you'd read a receipt. "It underperformed too."

"You regressed."

"Once. I was an Appraiser, and I was dying on a staircase, and a man with no face sat down next to me with a ledger on his knee and told me I had an unusual skill." She almost smiled. "He's very consistent. I'll give him that."

"What did you put up?"

"My husband."

The board ticked over above us. Somebody had bought a crate of arrows at forty-one.

"I didn't notice the last four words either," Vesper said. "*To be appraised at regression.* I woke up in our kitchen and the window told me what I'd pledged, and he was standing at the counter making coffee. I made the first installment. And the second. I missed the third by four hours." She took her right hand out of her pocket and turned her wrist so the lantern light caught it. Under the cuff of the coat, a gray cord was tied twice around her wrist and knotted. "The Lender took him. Retroactively. He was never there. And the Lender sold my paper, because that's what he does when the risk stops being worth the return. Ashgrave bought it at a discount. They collected me."

"You remember him," I said.

"No." She said it very simply. "I remember that there was a him. That's different. Seized people don't keep their memories. But I'm an Appraiser. I can still read the list." She opened a window and held it out flat on her palm, the way I'd shown Damon the credit note on the courthouse steps. It was Ashgrave's color. A dull, even gray.

**[Vesper Kaine — Seized Memories: 1,114 entries]**
**[Entry 2: Spouse (name, face, voice). Status: Seized.]**
**[Entry 3: Kitchen, Asset #61,207, morning. Status: Seized.]**
**[Entry 4: Coffee (preference). Status: Seized.]**

"I know I liked my coffee a certain way," she said, "because the list says it was taken. I drink it black now. I don't know if that's right."

I didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say that she hadn't already priced.

"That's what Day Thirty looks like for her," Vesper said, closing the window. "Not dying. Being filed. She'll stand in your Spire plaza with a cord on her wrist and look at you the way she looks at a stranger, and if you're very unlucky, she'll be good at the work. I was."

---

"Why are you telling me any of this?" I said.

"Because on Day Six I called her something you were holding, and you corrected me. Most people in your position argue about the price." She tipped her head toward the listings. "Do you know how seniority works, Mr. Marsh? Not on the board. In law."

"Tell me."

"A senior lien is first in line. Everyone else's claim on the same collateral sits underneath it. Junior." She said each word clearly, like an instructor. "If the senior holder forecloses, exercises the lien, takes the collateral, then every junior claim against that collateral is extinguished. Not reduced. Not delayed. Gone." She looked at me. "Ours. Your Lender's too."

I stood very still.

"Is that a warning?" I said.

"It's the law," said Vesper. "What you do with the law is your business. My business is collecting on Day Thirty at noon." She paused. "Whoever holds that senior lien, I'd very much like to know what they plan to do with it. My firm has had people trying to read it for a long time."

"How long?"

"Since we bought Loan Six. Paper comes with its file. It always does." She opened another gray window, and this one she didn't hold out. She turned it slowly so I could read it over her shoulder, the way the Lender had turned his ledger on the hundredth floor.

**[Loan Six — Default Record (Prior Loop)]**
**[Missed Installment: Year 9, Day 3, 12:00.]**
**[Borrower Seated: Year 9, Day 3, 23:40.]**
**[Escort: Field Agent S. Rook (Ashgrave Recoveries).]**
**[Outcome: Borrower's remaining existence transferred to third party (Private Lender) during seating. Firm's recovery: 0.]**

I read it twice. The third time I read only one line.

*Escort: Field Agent S. Rook.*

"The chair," I said.

"Every city we work has one. Ours is very plain. A defaulted borrower sits in it, and the firm reads him into the books." Vesper closed the window. "Your sixth sat down in our chair at twenty to midnight, and while we were reading him, he paid somebody else. Everything he had left. To the Lender. For a senior claim on the next version of his sister." She sounded, for the first time, almost admiring. "We've never lost a borrower in the chair before. The file calls it a recovery of zero. Somebody in accounting framed that line."

"And Silas walked him there."

"Silas Rook was our field agent in that city for three years. He was good at it. People trusted him. He'd been a guild master." She looked across the plaza toward the hotel on Fifth Street, where one window on the third floor was still lit. "He told you he left before Day Three. He didn't. He was the last person your sixth ever spoke to."

"And now," I said slowly, "you've got a new field agent."

"Damon Kreel signed with us on Day Thirteen." She said it without any weight at all. "He's very motivated. He knows your sister's habits. Where she sleeps. Which stairs she takes. Which side she favors when she's tired." She met my eyes. "He sells those to us by the line."

I'd known that. Knowing it in the abstract and hearing it said under the board were different things, and I found my hands had closed in my pockets.

"And you just let him," I said. "You can read him. You can see what he is."

"I can see his price. Eleven, the last time I looked." Vesper's mouth moved very slightly. "Mr. Marsh, I don't choose who the firm hires. A seized person doesn't choose much of anything. I get a list in the morning and I collect what's on it. Some mornings the list is short. Some mornings it has twenty-two names on it and a man I used to respect sitting on the steps outside." She glanced at her wrist. "The cord isn't a lock. It doesn't need to be. Where would I go? My world was sold six years ago. Every street I knew is in somebody else's inventory."

"You could stop."

"I could. And the firm would write me down to zero and sell me to someone worse, and someone worse would collect on Day Thirty instead of me." She said it without self-pity, the way I'd once told a guild treasurer that his sword was worth less than its scabbard. "I've done that arithmetic. So have you, I imagine, about other things."

I had. On a gravel roof, with a list of my own memories in front of me.

"Write it down," Vesper said. "That's what you do, isn't it? Write it down and do the arithmetic later." She stepped back out of the light. "Good night, Mr. Marsh."

---

I didn't go back to the hotel. I sat on the rim of the fountain between two sleeping strangers and wrote it all in the ledger in my slanted letters, every line I could remember from both windows, the times to the minute. Then I wrote:

*Senior forecloses → junior extinguished. Ashgrave = junior? Lender (#7, her) = junior?*

I looked at it for a long time. Then I closed the notebook, because I didn't understand it yet, and pretending I did was how men like me ended up in chairs.

Nora found me there at five in the morning. She'd brought two cups of instant coffee and the hotel blanket off her bed. She sat down, took the ledger out of my hands, read the new page, and didn't say anything for a while.

"Silas," she said finally.

"Silas."

She read it again. Her jaw moved. Then she uncapped her pen and, under my lines, in the Day Seventeen column, wrote *Hold the Line: 0 uses*, and handed the ledger back.

"He's at the Spire," she said. "He goes every morning at five-thirty and watches the door. Bram told me."

---

The plaza at the foot of the Spire was crowded already. Day Seventeen. The Kiln had been cleared a week ago, and everyone in the city with a weapon and a bad debt knew what the Exchange had promised: seven days after a floor's first clear, the next floor opens. Groups stood in loose knots around the black door, stamping their feet against the cold, checking gear they'd checked ten times already.

Silas was standing alone by the wall where Bram had sat on Day Six, wiping down the thin blade of his dueling sword with a rag that had been a shirt. He saw us coming. He saw my face. He didn't move.

"She told you," he said.

"Escort, Field Agent S. Rook," I said. "Twenty to midnight. You walked him to the chair."

Bram had come in behind us. Jonah and Owen behind him. I hadn't asked them to. Nobody stepped forward.

"Yes," Silas said.

"You sat in that hotel room and let her ask you what happened, and you told her about ten minutes and four seconds, and you cut yourself out of it."

"Yes."

"Why?"

He looked at Nora for a moment before he answered me.

"Because if I'd put myself in it," he said, "you'd have asked what he said on the walk. And I'd have had to tell you he didn't say anything. Two kilometers. Not one word. He held the door for me at the end." He leaned the sword against the wall. "I was Ashgrave's man for three years in that city. I brought them eleven borrowers. I was good at it. I thought I was the reasonable one. Somebody was always going to do the walking, and I was polite about it."

"And then?"

"And then I watched the chair read him and come up empty, and I watched the whole city decide he'd never existed, and I thought, I don't want to be the reasonable one anymore." He smiled without any humor at all. "So I found the man with no face, and I put up twenty-two people who trusted me, and I came back. To stand on the other side this time." He spread his hands. "And I lost again. I'm not offering an excuse, Marsh. I don't have one. I have a ledger, same as you. Mine just doesn't balance."

Nora stepped past me.

I thought she might hit him. Bram thought so too; I saw him shift his weight. She didn't. She looked up at Silas for a long time, the way she'd looked at the door line on her appraisal three times the day before.

"Two kilometers," she said. "And he held the door for you."

"Yes."

"He'd do that," she said. "He'd absolutely do that." She turned away. "You don't get to walk anybody anywhere again. Not him. Not me."

"No," Silas agreed.

Bram hadn't said anything the whole time. Now he walked over, picked Silas's sword up off the wall, looked at it, and handed it back to him hilt first.

"You kicked me out a day before the default," Bram said, "so I wouldn't be on the list. I've been grateful for eleven days. I'm going to keep being grateful. I'm just going to be angry at the same time." He shrugged his huge shoulders. "I can do both. I'm big."

Silas took the sword. For a second he looked like he might say something. He didn't.

Above us, the Spire's white surface flickered, and the gold came up across the sky over the plaza, and every head in the square tilted back at once.

**[REGIONAL NOTICE]**
**[Spire, Floor 2 (The Counting House): Open.]**
**[First Clear Bonus (Regional): 4,000 Merit.]**

The knots of people broke apart and surged toward the black door. Somebody whooped. Somebody else was already shouting about prices.

Day Seventeen. Six days to the third installment. Thirteen to the library.

"Tomorrow," I said to Nora. "We go in tomorrow. Today we finish the House's ledger and buy rope."

"Rope," she said.

"It's a counting house. Something in there is going to hang over a drop."

She wrote *rope* in the margin.

---

When I turned back toward the hotel, Vesper was standing at the edge of the crowd, exactly where the price tag is when you pick something up. She hadn't been there a moment before. She was watching the door, not me.

She waited until I'd almost passed her.

"One more thing, Mr. Marsh, and then we're square." She didn't turn her head. "My firm has hired someone to make sure you're not standing at that library at noon on Day Thirty. He doesn't know how you died on the hundredth floor. He wasn't there, not in the way that counts. But he'll use the same method anyway." Now she looked at me. "Some men only ever learn one trick."
