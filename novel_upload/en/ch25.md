# Chapter 25: The Other Side

"Free," I said.

"This once." The Lender crossed his legs. The chair was the color of old paper, the same as every window he'd ever opened in front of me, and it sat between the two hotel beds as if the room had been built around it. "Consider it customer service."

Nora was asleep in the other bed with one arm hanging off the edge. She'd fallen asleep in her boots after the courthouse, still wearing the hoodie with the burn hole on the cuff. She wasn't breathing. Not in the bad way. In the way the pigeon on the roof of Harlan Hall hadn't finished opening its wing.

The clock on the nightstand said 03:12. Day Twenty-Eight.

"Nothing's free," I said. "Not from you."

"Then appraise it. You always do."

I did.

**[Appraise]**

**[Offer: Viewing (Ledger Margin, Account #7) — Market Value: 0 Merit]**
**[True Value: 0 Merit]**
**[Hidden Clauses: None.]**

I read it three times. I'd never seen a window from him with no hidden clause. It made me trust it less.

"Why?" I said.

"Because there's something written in my margin that I can't read, Mr. Marsh, and it's been there since Year Nine, and it annoys me." His blank face tilted toward Nora. "And because on Day Thirty I'd like my borrower to make his decisions with all the information. I find that people who know what they're buying make better customers. It's a minority view in my line of work."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I go, and you sleep, and on Day Thirty you open a door without knowing who's behind it." He opened the ledger on his knee. "Your choice. You only have to look."

I looked at Nora. Her mouth was a little open. There was a scrape on her chin from the courthouse crowd, where somebody's elbow had caught her when the board ruled.

"Yes," I said.

He turned the ledger around so the page faced me, the way he had on the hundredth floor.

The page was very large. Then it was larger than the room.

---

I was standing on paper.

It went on in every direction, yellowed and soft, with a grain to it like the inside of an old book. The light came from nowhere in particular. It smelled of dust and iron, the smell of a library basement and a cut finger at the same time.

Far off, in every direction I turned, there were lines. Faded red lines, ruled perfectly straight, running to the edge of what I could see. They looked like horizons. There were seven of them, stacked one above the next across the sky, and the writing along them was so big that I had to turn my head to read a single letter.

*Accounts — Borrower: Marsh, E.*

The page from the Librarian's desk. The one I'd thrown into the Assayer's crucible on Day Ten and watched burn. Here it wasn't burned. Here it was the whole world.

The first five lines had been scraped. Not crossed out. Scraped, the way you scrape a mistake off parchment with a knife, until the paper is thin and furred and you can almost see through it. Where the words had been there were only pale scars a mile long.

The seventh line was bright and wet. *Loan #7 — Collateral: Nora Marsh. Status: Active.* I could see the ink moving on it. Interest, I guessed. Accruing.

The sixth line was where the margin started.

There was a chair there. An ordinary wooden one, from a kitchen. Somebody was sitting in it with his back to me, writing on the paper floor beside him with his left hand.

"You came," he said, without turning around. "I wasn't sure he'd let you."

It was my voice.

The same voice with something taken out of it, the way a coat loses its shape after enough winters.

He stood up and turned around.

He was thirty-four. I knew that without appraising him, because I could count. Same height as me. Same bad posture, the left shoulder a little lower from years of carrying ledgers on one side. His hair was going gray at the temples in two clean stripes. He had a scar through his right eyebrow that I didn't have yet, and lines at the corners of his eyes that I didn't have yet either, and his left hand was black to the wrist. Ink had soaked into the skin of his fingers and his palm and crawled up past the knuckles, as if he'd been writing with his hand instead of a pen for a very long time.

"Hi," he said.

"Hi."

We looked at each other. I'd thought about this every night since I'd read the ledger wall in the ninth alcove and recognized my own *t*s. I had about forty questions. None of them came out.

"You look young," he said finally.

"You look tired."

"I am tired." He smiled. It was my smile, the one Nora said made me look like I was apologizing for something. "Nine years and then whatever this is. I don't know how long it's been in here. There aren't any days in a margin."

---

He didn't have a second chair, so we sat on the paper, the way Nora and I used to sit on the floor of the lounge in Harlan Hall. Up close I could read what he'd been writing. It was arithmetic. Columns of it, in a cramped hand that leaned hard to the right, the *t*s crossed too high. The same sum over and over, with the numbers slightly changed each time, as if he'd been checking it.

"Can I appraise you?" I said.

"You'll do it anyway."

**[Appraise]**

**[Eli Marsh (Loop 6) — Human — Appraiser (Lv. 58)]**
**[Status: Recorded. Not present.]**
**[Market Value: — ]**
**[True Value: — ]**
**[Held as: Senior Lien (Bearer Form), Collateral: Nora Marsh (next account).]**

"Recorded," I said. "Not present."

"That's about right." He rubbed his black thumb against his fingers. "I'm not a person anymore. I'm a line item. A right. You can't appraise a right the way you appraise a man. You can only read what it's for."

"Tell me what happened."

"Rook told you."

"Rook told me his version."

He was quiet for a while. Somewhere far off along the seventh line, the ink kept moving.

"Floor eighty-eight," he said. "The Auditor. It goes after whoever owes the most. That was me. It was always going to be me, I'd been borrowing against everything for nine years to keep the Exchange from liquidating us, I owed more than the rest of the expedition put together." He looked at his hand. "Nora held the line. She didn't ask. She just said *sorry* and did it. Ten minutes. The best Warden on Earth, and she held it for ten minutes, and then she didn't."

"And your installment was due at noon."

"Noon. Same day." He laughed, not really. "I spent every Merit I had on her. Draughts. The rite at the temple on Ninth Avenue, twice, at three percent. It didn't take. By the time I looked at the clock, it was two in the afternoon, and Ashgrave already had the paper."

"And then you went to the chair."

"Rook walked me there. Don't be hard on him. He thought he was being kind." He paused. "No. Be a little hard on him. He'd want that more."

---

"Loans one to five," I said, and nodded up at the scraped lines. "What were they?"

"Us." He said it simply. "All of them put up their own existence. The way the draft says. *Collateral: Borrower's Existence.* None of them haggled. Or they haggled the interest and left the collateral alone. They defaulted somewhere between Day Three and Year Two, and the Lender took them, retroactively, and everything they'd done went with them." He looked at the scars across the sky. "That's why you don't remember them. That's why I don't. There's nothing left to remember. I only know they were there because the lines are."

"And you."

"I was the first one who argued about the collateral." He looked at me sideways. "You did the same thing, didn't you. *A borrower with nothing to protect is a bad borrower.*"

"Word for word."

"Then he said *your most valuable possession*, and we both said yes, and we both missed the last four words." He shook his head. "I've had a long time to think about those four words."

I said, "Did you ever tell her?"

He didn't answer right away.

"No," he said. "Nine years. I never told her what I put up. She knew there was a Lender. She knew I had installments. She thought the collateral was me. I let her think it. I told myself it was to protect her, and maybe it was, the first year. After that it was just easier." His black hand closed. "She found out on the eighty-eighth floor. The Auditor reads its targets' debts out loud before it moves. It read mine. It got to the line with her name on it, and she turned around and looked at me, and that's when she said sorry. Then she held the line."

The paper under us was very quiet.

"You told her," he said. "I watched it through the margin. I can see the seventh line from here, sometimes, when the ink's fresh. You told her, and she was furious, and then she made you show her the numbers." He looked at me. "Out of seven of us, you're the only one who told her. Do you know that?"

"She had to ask me first."

"She asked me too. Lots of times." He almost smiled. "I had nine years to answer."

---

I made myself ask the practical questions, because that was the only way I was going to get through the rest of it.

"The lien," I said. "Explain it to me like I'm buying it."

He brightened a little. It was an awful thing to see on my own face, the way a man brightens when someone asks about his work.

"When I went to the chair, I had one asset left. Me. What was left of me after the debt. I couldn't save her, that account was closed. So I registered a claim against the next one." He drew a box on the paper with his finger, and the ink followed it. "A senior lien against Nora Marsh, effective on any later account. Senior means it comes first. Anybody who lends against her afterward is junior to me. Ashgrave's paper on Loan Six. His claim on Loan Seven. All junior."

"And when a senior holder forecloses—"

"Every junior claim is extinguished." He tapped the box. "Vesper told you that, I think. She'd know. It's how they lost her planet."

"Why scratch out the name?"

"Because I couldn't exercise it. I'm not present. A lien that only I could use would just sit here forever." He drew a second box, smaller. "So I put it in bearer form. Anyone holding the right name, with a live private account, standing at the door, can exercise it. Same name, live account, present. That's you. That's only ever going to be you."

"And the door."

"The door is the only place I can reach. Hale Memorial. Where she first held the line, in every loop. I carved the warning on it from this side, a little at a time. It took me what felt like years." He looked at his hand. "Same with the Kiln wall. You'd be surprised how hard it is to write through a margin. Everything comes out backwards first."

"Day Thirty at noon."

"That's when Ashgrave collects on Six. The lien has to be exercised at the moment of foreclosure, or there's nothing to foreclose against. At noon I open the door from my side. You stand on yours. You say the words."

"And then what happens to you?"

He looked at me as if I'd asked something very obvious.

"A lien that's been exercised is released," he said. "I'm the lien."

"You disappear."

"I get to stop." He said it gently. "That's not the same thing, from in here."

I wanted to argue. I opened my mouth to do it. He held up his black hand, and I shut it, because it was exactly the gesture I made at Nora when she was about to tell me I was being stupid, and I'd never seen it from the outside before.

"I want this," he said. "I've wanted it since the chair. Don't take it off me by being kind."

---

He stood up, and so did I, and we walked a little way along the sixth line. It ran under our feet like a road. *Loan #6 — Collateral: Nora Marsh. Status: Defaulted. Collection pending.* The letters were each the size of a car.

"Now the part you came for," he said. "The part he didn't want to tell you himself."

"The warning."

"Loan Seven's collateral is Nora, by substitution. *Your most valuable possession, appraised at regression.* The original clause was *Collateral: Borrower's Existence.* That's still written underneath." He crouched and pointed. Under the bright wet ink of the seventh line, faint, like a watermark, there was older text. "When you foreclose, the lien wipes every junior claim against her. Including his. Loan Seven loses its collateral."

"And reverts."

"To the original term. Your existence." He stood up. "And then it's an unsecured loan to a man whose collateral just vanished, and he's going to call it. Immediately. Not because he's cruel. Because that's what you do with paper like that."

I did the math out loud, because I couldn't help it. "Ten thousand four hundred and eighteen on Day Twenty-Three. One and a half a day. Seven days. Times one point oh one five to the seventh." I worked it through. "Eleven thousand five hundred and sixty-two. On Day Thirty."

"You'll have about four hundred."

"Four hundred and nineteen, if the stall has a good week."

"Then you'll need someone to buy it." He looked at me steadily. "That's what I'm telling you. When he calls it, there has to be a buyer standing there. Someone who can pay, or someone who can take the note off his hands. I didn't have one. Nobody bought for me. That's the whole story of my account, if you want it in a line. *No buyer.*"

"I'll buy it."

"With what?"

"I don't know yet." I heard my own voice and didn't like how young it sounded. "I'll buy it. I'll find the money or I'll find the trade. That's the only thing I've ever been good at."

He looked at me a long time.

"Yeah," he said. "Me too. For about nine years."

---

The paper had started to fade at the edges. I could feel the hotel room behind it, the way you can feel a wall behind you in the dark.

"He's calling you back," Eli said. The other Eli. I didn't know what else to call him.

"Wait. One more."

"Go on."

"What do I tell her? Nora. About you."

He took a long time with that.

"Tell her I said not to hold the line," he said. "She'll hate it. She'll do it anyway, probably. But tell her." He turned his black hand over and looked at the palm. "And tell her I'm sorry about the bakery. In my loop, it closed in Year Five. I never took her."

"Hollis Street."

"Hollis Street. They make these cinnamon things."

"I know."

"Of course you know." He smiled, my smile, the apologizing one. "Noon on Day Thirty. When I'm ready, I'll knock."

"How will I know it's you?"

He raised his black hand and rapped it on the air between us, where nothing was. There was no sound. I heard it anyway.

Two quick. A pause. One slow.

"It was hers first," he said. "I borrowed it. Everything I have is borrowed."

The margin went white.

---

I woke up sitting on the end of my bed with my feet on the carpet.

The chair was gone. The clock said 03:12. Not a second had moved.

Nora turned over and said something into the pillow that might have been *stop thinking so loud.* I looked at my hands. Both clean. Both mine.

*Somebody has to buy.*

I did the sum again. Eleven thousand five hundred and sixty-two. Four hundred and nineteen. Even if I sold every item the party owned, every share of the stall, the coat off my back, I'd be more than ten thousand short. Nothing I owned was worth that.

Nothing I owned.

I sat very still.

Then I opened my own status and scrolled down past my level and my titles, to the line I hadn't looked at since the white room on Day Zero. You don't check the price of the house you're living in.

**[Appraiser — Market Value: 5 Merit | True Value: 41,000 Merit]**
**[Condition: Ledger-Bound. Value realized only with an active private account.]**

Forty-one thousand.

I'd had it for twenty-eight days. I'd had it, in a way, for seven years before that. The best class in the world, and I'd never had a buyer.

I'd never once tried to sell it.
