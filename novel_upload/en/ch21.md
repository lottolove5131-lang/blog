# Chapter 21: Shareholders

The gray mark didn't come out.

Owen tried water on the night of Day Twenty, and soap on the morning of Day Twenty-One, and finally a rag soaked in the cheap Exchange solvent that people used to get monster ichor off their boots. The stain sat across Nora's armor from collarbone to hip like a line drawn through a figure in a ledger. It wasn't dirt. It was a record.

**[Appraise]**

**[Warden's Brigandine (Repaired) — Market Value: 140 Merit | True Value: 180 Merit]**
**[Mark: "Collected in kind, Floor 2, Day 20." Permanent.]**

"It's ugly," Nora said, holding it up to the hotel window. Her left arm was still bound to her side. Owen had set it twice and healed it four times, and it was almost right, but she couldn't lift the axe yet. "I look like a returned item."

"You look like someone who held a window for three minutes," Bram said from the doorway. He'd slept on the floor outside our room, across the doorway, both nights. Nobody had asked him to.

I appraised Nora again. I'd been doing it every few hours since the stairs, since Jonah crouched next to me and repeated Damon's words in that flat voice. *The impairment's on record, right?*

**[Nora Marsh — Human — Warden (Lv. 17)]**
**[Market Value: 3,900 Merit]**
**[True Value: Pledged (Prior lien: E█i M█rsh) (Loan 6: Ashgrave Recoveries) (Loan 7: Private Lender)]**
**[Loan 6: Collection — Day 30.]**

Day Thirty. Still Day Thirty. Whatever Ashgrave had written in its gray book, it wasn't in any book I could read.

"Anything?" Nora asked.

"Your market value went up again."

"How much?"

"You're worth more than the hotel."

"The hotel's worth negative forty a week," Owen said, without looking up from his clipboard. "You told me yesterday."

He wasn't wrong. I'd appraised the lease the night I took it.

**[Headquarters Lease (Merchant Row Hotel) — Market Value: -40 Merit/week]**
**[Note: Rent arrears assumed with lease.]**

Damon had been paying for the lobby with Vanguard's interest money, and then with Ashgrave's. Now I owned a lobby with fifteen empty rooms above it and a bill. We'd carried our bags over from Fifth Street the same night, because if I was paying for the rooms, somebody might as well sleep in them. I'd given the six packs back to the fifteen on the morning of Day Twenty-One, one plate and one ingot bar at a time, to whoever's body they'd been fitted to. They'd been billed four hundred apiece holding still for my sister's sake. It seemed fair that they kept their armor.

That left me with the ledger, and with the thing Jonah had heard.

I told Nora on the morning of Day Twenty-One, before the stall opened, because that was the deal now. All the numbers. Both of us. I told her the exact words, and where Damon had been standing, and who he'd said them to.

She listened with her bound arm across her chest. When I finished she was quiet for a while.

"So I made it worse," she said. "Ringing the bell. That's what he wanted. I did exactly what was in the file."

"You did."

"Great."

"You also kept Owen breathing," I said. "And Jonah. And me. The file had that part too. Damon just didn't think it was worth anything."

She looked at the gray stripe on her armor where it hung on the chair. "What's impairment for? What does it buy them?"

"I don't know yet."

"Find out," she said. "Before they spend it."

That night she opened her half of the ledger and changed one line. She didn't ask me to look. I looked anyway.

*Hold the Line: 1.*


---

I did it on Day Twenty-Two, at the Clipboard House stall in the courthouse square, at the end of the day, with the board's gold light coming down over the stones and Owen counting the take.

The stall was two folding tables and a sign. Maya had lettered the sign for us on the back of a cafeteria tray, in careful black capitals: *CLIPBOARD HOUSE — FAIR APPRAISAL — 2% FEE.* The square charged three Merit a day for the patch of stone under the table. Owen and I split everything down the middle, rent included, and he wrote down both halves.

"Two hundred ten," Owen said. "Your share. Day Twenty-One and Twenty-Two together. It was busy."

It had been. Word had gone around about Floor Two, about a guild master who'd designated his own predecessor and then voided everyone's contracts, and people had come to look at the stall the way they'd come to look at the Kiln parties on Day Six. Some of them had brought contracts. Three of the fifteen had come with their old Vanguard agreements, the voided ones, and asked me to read them anyway, just to hear it. I'd read them for free.

**[Balance (Eli): 2,859 + 210 = 3,069 Merit]**
**[Third Installment: 3,500 Merit. Due: Day 23, 12:00.]**

"Three thousand five hundred," I said, "minus three thousand sixty-nine."

"Four hundred thirty-one," Owen said, before I could.

"Four thirty-one. Eighteen hours."

Jonah was sitting on an upturned crate, restringing his bow. Bram was holding up the awning pole, which didn't need holding up. Nora sat on the stall's edge with her arm in its sling.

"Private sale," Jonah said. "Something. Anything. You found a twenty-percent buyer for everything else."

"I've got nothing to sell. The Scales went two days ago. My one sale every twelve hours is open, and the most valuable thing in my inventory is a dagger the Lender would buy for four Merit." I didn't say the other thing. There were other things the Lender bought. I'd sold him my mother's face on Day Three for ninety-one Merit, and I didn't know what it had looked like anymore, and I didn't know what else I had left that was worth four hundred.

Nora knew what I wasn't saying. Nora always knew.

"No," she said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You thought about it. You've got the face." She turned to Owen. "He's got the face, right? The roof face."

"He's got the roof face," Owen confirmed.

"Then I'll borrow it," I said. "Not from any of you. Somewhere. The Credit Market lends against—"

"Against what?" Jonah said. "You don't own anything. You own a dormant guild and a hotel that costs money."

"I own a ledger."

"Eli." Bram let go of the awning pole. It stayed up fine. "We'll lend it. A hundred and ten each. Done. You pay it back when you pay it back."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because I've got one lender already," I said. "And I know exactly what it's like to owe him. I'm not going to owe you. I'm not going to wake up on Day Thirty-Three needing four thousand and owe the four people who stood next to me in the Counting House on top of it. I'm not putting any of you on a schedule."

Nobody said anything for a while. The board flickered over our heads. Someone at the next stall was selling roast pigeon, and the smell of it was everywhere.

Then Nora got up off the stall's edge, walked around behind Owen, and took his clipboard out of his hands.

---

"Hey," Owen said.

"Borrowing it." She turned it over. The back was bare pressboard, scuffed. She held out her good hand. "Pen."

He gave her the pen. He'd never in his life refused anyone a pen.

She wrote for a minute, with her tongue in the corner of her mouth the way she used to do homework at the kitchen table, and then turned it around and held it up in front of my face like I'd held the Credit Note up in front of Damon's.

*CLIPBOARD HOUSE — SHAREHOLDER REGISTER*

*Founders: Owen Park, Eli Marsh.*

*Shares issued, Day 22: four (4), at 110 Merit each.*

*Each share: one-tenth of Eli Marsh's portion of the House's take, from Day 23 forward. Not callable. No interest. No installments. Transferable only by agreement of all holders.*

"I'm not lending you anything," Nora said. "I'm buying. There's a difference. You taught me the difference. A loan you pay back. A share you just—have. If the House does well, I do well. If it does badly, that's my problem, because I bought badly."

"Nora."

"It's a good stall, Eli. I've watched the line. I did the math." She tapped the clipboard. "Two hundred ten in two days, your share. That's about a hundred and five a day. One-tenth is ten and a half. I get my money back in eleven days, and then I just keep getting it." She looked at me sideways. "Unless you're telling me the House is a bad investment. You can tell me. You're the appraiser."

I appraised the clipboard. I couldn't help it.

**[Shareholder Register (Draft) — Market Value: 0 Merit | True Value: 440 Merit]**

Not the paper. The paper was worth nothing. The four signatures that weren't on it yet were worth exactly what they were paying.

"It's a fair price," I said finally.

"Good," said Nora. "Owen, are you a founder or not?"

"I'm a founder." He took the clipboard back and read it. He frowned. He took out a second pen, a red one, and added a line at the bottom in small square letters.

*Witnessed and recorded: O. Park.*

"Now it's real," he said.

Jonah signed first. A hundred and ten Merit went out of his account and into mine with a soft chime. Bram signed with his whole fist around the pen, so the letters came out enormous, and then wrote, under his name, *equal fifths*, and underlined it, though it didn't mean anything on a share register. Owen signed his own register with a straight face. Nora signed last, left-handed, because her right was bound, and the signature looked like a seismograph.

**[Balance (Eli): 3,069 + 440 = 3,509 Merit]**

"Nine over," I said.

"Nine over," Nora agreed, and handed me the clipboard. "Don't spend it."

---

On Day Twenty-Three at 11:52, I sat on the Clipboard House stall with my ledger open and waited.

The Lender's window opened at noon, the color of old paper, in the air next to my shoulder.

**[LOAN ACCOUNT — INSTALLMENT DUE]**
**[Third Installment: 3,500 Merit.]**
**[Pay now? Y / N]**

"Yes."

**[Third Installment: PAID.]**
**[Balance: 9 Merit]**

**[LOAN ACCOUNT — STATEMENT]**
**[Outstanding Principal: 10,418 Merit (interest accrued at 1.5% daily).]**
**[Fourth Installment: 4,000 Merit. Due: Day 33, 12:00.]**

I did the check in my head, because I always did. Eleven thousand nine hundred ninety-three on Day Thirteen. Ten days at one and a half percent compounding was a multiplier of about one point one six. Thirteen thousand nine hundred and eighteen. Minus thirty-five hundred. Ten thousand four hundred and eighteen. The Lender's arithmetic had never once been wrong. That was the worst thing about him.

I closed the window and the chair was there.

It hadn't been there a second ago. It never was. It sat on the stones beside the stall, a plain wooden office chair with a cracked leather seat, and the Lender was sitting in it with his ledger on his knees and his blank oval face turned toward the square, as if he'd come to watch the pigeons.

"Mr. Marsh," he said. "Congratulations on another installment. You're becoming one of my more reliable accounts. That's not a compliment, exactly. Reliable accounts are boring."

"You don't usually come in person."

"I wanted to see the stall." His face tilted toward the shareholder register on my knee. "Equity. How modern of you. Most of my debtors, when they run short, go to their friends for a loan, and then I have the pleasure of watching the friends become creditors. You sold them a piece of yourself instead." He sounded genuinely pleased, the way he had on the hundredth floor when I'd haggled him down to one and a half. "That's very good. I'd have advised against it, of course. But it's very good."

Nora was standing ten feet away, at the end of the stall, with Bram. She couldn't see him. I knew that. She was watching me talk to an empty piece of air. But her eyes had found the chair, or the place where the chair would be, the same way they had on Day Thirteen.

The Lender turned his face toward her. He looked at her for a while.

"The collateral has appreciated considerably," he said. "A level-seventeen Warden. Two first clears. Four regional notices. A very public three minutes on the second floor, I hear." He made a note in the ledger. "The market has started to look at her, Mr. Marsh. When the market starts looking, it starts making offers. I thought you'd want to know."

"She's not for sale."

"Everything's for sale. That's your only rule, as I recall." He turned a page. "And she is, technically, already sold. Several times. In order of priority."

"The prior lien."

"Ah." His voice did something I'd never heard it do. It went flat, like a man stepping on a stair that wasn't there. "Yes. That."

"What is it to you?"

"An inconvenience," the Lender said. "A very old instrument, filed in a very odd way, by a borrower who should have known better. It sits in my books where I can't touch it. Inconvenient instruments are bad for business, Mr. Marsh. I've learned to live with a great many of them." He closed the ledger. "Pay on time. Read the fine print. You're very good at both. Good afternoon."

The chair was empty. Then it wasn't there.

---

That night, I couldn't sleep.

Bram was across the door again. Nora was in the other bed with her arm propped on a pillow, breathing slow. The hotel room smelled of Owen's solvent and the roast pigeon somebody had brought back for dinner. My balance was nine Merit and my shareholder register was in my coat pocket, and I lay on top of the sheets and looked at the ceiling and thought about the word *inconvenient.*

Then I sat up and appraised my sister one more time. Not her. The line.

**[Appraise — Prior Lien]**

I'd done it a dozen times since the first night. It had always given me the same thing: sealed, holder unreadable, *shaped like a door.* Vesper had told me about the door. The handwriting on the Kiln wall had told me *be the bearer*, and I'd underlined that in my ledger without understanding it, and gone on without understanding it, because there'd always been an installment closer than Day Thirty.

**[Prior Lien — Holder: E█i M█rsh]**
**[Recorded: Year 9, Day 3]**
**[Status: Sealed. Opens from the other side, Day 30.]**

And under that, small, in letters the color of old paper, a line I had never seen before. As if the window had decided I could finally afford to read it.

**[Form: Bearer.]**
**[Exercise requirements: Same name. Active private account. Present at the door.]**

I read it four times.

Same name. The name on the lien was half erased. Two letters gone. *E█i M█rsh.* It had never said Eli Marsh the Sixth or the man from Year Nine or any of the other things I'd been calling him in my head. It had only ever said the name, with holes in it.

Active private account. I had one. Nine Merit in it and ten thousand four hundred and eighteen owed.

Present at the door.

I looked across the room at my sister, asleep with a gray mark on her armor where a window had billed her for me.

"…I'm an E█i M█rsh too," I said.
