# Chapter 29: Buyer

**[00:59:40]**

The first time I'd met him, I was lying on my back with a slab of white stone across my legs and forty-six minutes left to live.

This time I sat down.

Not because I had to. My legs worked fine. I sat on the top step of Hale Memorial Library, facing the chair, close enough that our knees almost touched, because that was how you did business with this man. You got down to the height of the ledger.

"You have about fifty-nine minutes, Mr. Marsh," the Lender said. "I wouldn't want to waste them."

"Neither would I." I put my hands on my knees so he could see they weren't shaking. They were, a little. "I've got something to sell."

Behind me, Nora said, "Who are you talking to?"

I held my hand out flat and turned the red-bordered window so it faced her. You couldn't show anyone your status. You could show them a notice. She read it. I watched her lips move on *retroactively*, and then her face went still in a way I'd only ever seen on our mother, in a photograph I couldn't remember.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. What are you selling?"

"The only thing I've got that's worth more than eleven thousand Merit."

---

I'd known since the morning of Day Twenty-Eight. I'd woken up in the hotel room with the yellow margin still behind my eyes, and the first thing in my head had been a number, the way numbers always come first for me.

I opened it now. Not my condition. Not my memories. I'd learned on a roof on Day Three what happened when you went looking in there. I appraised the thing I'd picked off a white menu thirty days ago because it was the one class that didn't ask me to fight.

**[Appraise]**

**[Appraiser (Class) — Market Value: 5 Merit | True Value: 41,000 Merit]**
**[Condition: Ledger-Bound. Value realized only with an active private account.]**

Five, and forty-one thousand.

On Day Zero I'd looked at that line and thought: the best class in the world, and I'd held it for seven years without a buyer. I'd thought it was a joke about me. It wasn't. It was a price tag with a condition on it, and the condition was sitting in a chair in front of me with a pen in his hand.

An active private account. I had one for about fifty-nine more minutes.

"Private sale," I said. "Twenty percent of True Value. The usual terms."

The Lender didn't move.

It was a very small thing. On the roof of Harlan Hall, he'd offered me my mother's face before I'd finished asking. On the hundredth floor, he'd laughed. I had never once, in either life, seen him take longer than a breath to answer an offer.

He took three.

"That," he said finally, "was my eye, originally."

"I know."

"You don't. You've guessed." He tapped the pen once against the ledger. "Ledger-Bound isn't a skill the Exchange hands out, Mr. Marsh. Every Appraiser on this planet got one number. You got two because I lent you the second. It came with the account. It was always going to go back with the account."

"Then buy it back," I said. "At list."

He tilted the blank oval of his face at me.

"You'd be selling it at the exact moment it's worth the most," he said slowly. "And the exact moment it stops being worth anything. When your account closes, the condition fails, and the second number goes dark whether I buy it or not."

"Which is why you'll pay twenty percent instead of zero." I leaned forward. "It's a perishable asset, and there's one buyer, and there's a clock. You taught me that one."

Somewhere down the steps, Jonah laughed out loud, one short bark, and then shut up.

The Lender sat very still for one more second.

Then he wrote.

**[Private Sale: Appraiser (Class) — True Value Component.]**
**[Merit Earned: 8,200 (20% of 41,000).]**
**[Delivery: upon closure of Private Account.]**
**[Balance: 8,619 Merit]**

"Four hundred and nineteen," I said, "plus eight thousand two hundred. Eight thousand six hundred and nineteen."

"Which leaves you two thousand nine hundred and forty-three short," the Lender said. "With fifty-two minutes. You'll forgive me if I don't extend further credit. Individuals are a business of much smaller margins."

"Much better conversation," I said. "I remember."

Two thousand nine hundred and forty-three. It was exactly what the salve had made me on the morning of Day Six, sitting on a hotel floor laughing at Damon's guild account. I didn't believe in omens. I believed in arithmetic, and arithmetic is mostly the same numbers coming around again.

"I'm not paying the balance," I said.

"Then I'm afraid—"

"I'm buying the paper."

---

Here's something the Lender had told me on Day Six, at the edge of the courthouse square, while I was still a hundred and sixty-eight short of my second installment. I'd written it down that night in the spiral notebook I'd bought for two Merit on Day Four, because by then I wrote down everything he said.

*I sell paper when the risk stops being worth the return.*

He'd sold Loan Six to Ashgrave. He'd sold it at a discount, quickly, to whoever would pay. That was his business. He didn't collect on everything personally. It wasn't efficient.

"Loan Seven," I said. "It's called, it's unsecured, and the collateral is a man who has eight thousand six hundred Merit and fifty-two minutes. If he defaults, you seize his existence retroactively, and then there's nothing to sell, because there was never anything there. You'll have spent seven loops on the same borrower and walked away with a blank page." I opened my hand toward him. "Sell me the note."

"To the borrower."

"To a buyer. You don't care who."

**[Appraise]**

**[Credit Note: Loan #7 (Called, Unsecured) — Market Value: 1,840 Merit | True Value: 11,562 Merit]**

Eighteen hundred and forty. That was what the market thought a called loan to a desperate man was worth. About sixteen cents on the Merit. The market wasn't wrong, either. Inside the library doorway, Vesper Kaine had her arms folded and her eyes on the window, and I could see her doing the same math I was doing, and arriving at the same price. Ashgrave would have bid it.

"Fifteen hundred," I said.

"Seventeen."

"Fifteen hundred," I said, "and the interest starts after I buy it. Not now."

The Lender went perfectly still.

Then he laughed. The same laugh as the hundredth floor, warm and surprised, like a man who'd put his hand in an old coat and found money. It went on for a while. It was the only time I ever heard him sound young.

Because it was a joke, and he knew it. Interest from the moment I owned the note was interest I'd owe to myself.

"Sold," said the Lender.

**[Credit Note: Loan #7 — PURCHASED.]**
**[Holder: Eli Marsh.]**
**[Borrower: Eli Marsh.]**
**[Creditor and Debtor are the same party. Loan #7 extinguished by merger.]**
**[PRIVATE ACCOUNT — CLOSED.]**
**[Balance: 7,119 Merit]**
**[Time Remaining on Call: — (Void)]**

"Eight thousand six hundred and nineteen," I said, "minus fifteen hundred. Seven thousand one hundred and nineteen."

The red-bordered window folded itself in half and then in half again and was gone.

Behind me, Nora sat down hard on the step. I heard her do it. I didn't turn around, because I could feel the second number starting to go, a pressure behind my eyes like the moment before a sneeze, and I had maybe a few seconds of it left.

I wasn't going to waste them.

---

**[Appraise]**

**[The Lender — Market Value: N/A]**
**[True Value: Long position on Earth (Asset #88,412-C).]**

I read it twice. Then I looked up at the blank oval where his face should have been.

"You bet on us," I said.

"Someone has to take the other side of a trade, Mr. Marsh." He closed his ledger. "The Exchange bought your world expecting to sell it for parts in seven years. I don't share the Exchange's opinion. I rarely do. We're in the same industry, and we don't like each other." He capped his pen. "Seven loans to the same man. Very bad lending, if you were lending. Rather good, if you were buying."

"Buying what?"

"Futures." He stood, and lifted the chair by its back with one hand, the way you'd fold up a seat at the end of a long day. "Good luck, Mr. Marsh. It was a pleasure. As always."

Then the place where the light didn't reach was just shade again, and the landing was empty.

I had one second left. Maybe two.

I turned it on myself.

**[Eli Marsh — Human — Appraiser (Lv. 16)]**
**[Market Value: 3 Merit]**
**[True Value: Not for sale.]**

Three. Still three. On the hundredth floor that had been the worst number I'd ever read.

I was still looking at the line under it when it went out, the way a streetlight goes out at dawn, without any fuss.

**[Ledger-Bound: INACTIVE.]**

I blinked. The world looked the same. That was the strange part. The library, the steps, the gray sky. Everything still had a price on it, floating at the edge of my vision the way it always had. One price each. The way it was for everyone.

---

"Mr. Marsh."

Vesper had come out onto the landing. Up close, she looked tired, and older than thirty, and there was a fleck of white paper on her coat sleeve from the folder.

"Ashgrave has written off Loan Six," she said. "Total loss. First one in the firm's history, on this planet." She held out a gray window, and for the first time I could only read half of it. "Which leaves us holding paper we bought for a single day's collection. The Cinder Pact member agreements. Twenty-two of them, from Mr. Rook's default. The firm doesn't carry inventory it can't move, and I'm authorized to clear it at write-down."

"Which is what?"

"One hundred per agreement."

I looked down the steps. Twenty-two people in a ragged line, gray cords on their wrists, eyes like windows with nobody home. Bram still standing in front of them with his shield lowered now, almost touching the short woman with the burn scar.

"Twenty-two times a hundred," I said. "Twenty-two hundred. Seven thousand one hundred and nineteen, minus twenty-two hundred."

"Four thousand nine hundred and nineteen," said Owen, from the bottom of the stairs, already writing it down.

"Done," I said.

**[Member Agreements (Cinder Pact) x22 — PURCHASED. Holder: Eli Marsh.]**
**[Balance: 4,919 Merit]**

I didn't read them. I didn't have to. I'd been reading fine print for thirty days and seven years, and I knew what I was going to do with them before the window finished opening.

"All twenty-two," I said. "Void."

The cords didn't snap. They just weren't there anymore, the way the thread had gone from Nora's wrist. And the twenty-two people on the steps did the same thing at the same time, which was blink, as if somebody had turned on a light in a room they'd been sitting in for three weeks.

The short woman with the burn scar was the first to say anything. She looked up at the man in front of her. Two and a half meters of scorched armor and a shield as tall as I was.

"Bram?" she said. "Why are you crying?"

Bram didn't answer. He just put his shield down on the steps.

Silas Rook walked down past me. He stopped in front of Bram, and in front of all of them, the twenty-two he'd pledged and lost and come back for, and he bent at the waist until his head was lower than the top of Bram's shield. He held it there for a long time. Nobody told him to stop.

"Get up," Bram said at last. His voice was thick. "Not to me. I got out a day early. You did that." He jerked his chin at the line behind him. "Say it to them."

Silas straightened. He turned around to face the twenty-two, and he opened his mouth, and for the first time since I'd met him, the man who'd lived to Year Nine didn't have anything ready.

"Hi," he said. "It's been three weeks. I'll explain."

The woman with the burn scar looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked at Bram. "Can he?"

"Badly," said Bram. "Let him try."

---

Silas had let Damon up at some point. I found him at the bottom of the steps, mud down the front of his armor, walking away across the field, his own gray cord still knotted on his wrist. He'd tied it himself. Voiding Ashgrave's paper didn't untie anything he'd chosen.

"Damon."

He stopped. He didn't turn around.

His Ashgrave contract had been a commission on Loan Six. Loan Six didn't exist. There was nothing to commission. He'd sold my sister's daily routine to a collection agency for three weeks, and he'd end the day with exactly what he'd had in the morning.

I appraised him. One number. That was all I had now.

**[Damon Kreel — Human — Warrior (Lv. 15)]**
**[Market Value: 14 Merit]**

"Still fourteen," I said.

He stood there a moment longer. Then he kept walking, and didn't say anything, and I let him.

---

When I came back up, Vesper was waiting at the top with something small between two fingers.

"I've been transferred," she said. "Effective today. Head office doesn't like to keep agents on a planet where they've taken a loss." She held it out. A business card, heavy stock, the color of ash. *V. Kaine. Ashgrave Recoveries.* On the back, in very neat pen: *To the first one who made the ledger take a loss.*

"Where will they send you?"

"Somewhere that's underperforming." She almost smiled. "There's always one."

I looked at her the way I'd looked at her for thirty days, out of habit. I got her market value, and nothing else. Wherever her other line was, the one that said what Ashgrave had pledged her for, I couldn't read it anymore.

"I'm sorry," I said. "I can't see it now."

"I know. That's why I'm giving you a card instead." She walked down the steps and across the mud, the gray cord still on her wrist. Writing off one loan didn't release her own.

---

Nora was still sitting on the top step when I turned around. She had her phone out, the screen bright, her thumb resting on a photo I already knew. A woman on a dock, squinting into the sun.

She looked up at me. Her eyes were red, and she was furious about it.

"Eli," she said. "Tomorrow we're going to see Mom. And this time you're not late."
