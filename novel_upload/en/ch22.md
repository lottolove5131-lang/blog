# Chapter 22: Bearer

I said it out loud a second time, to make sure it was still true.

"I'm E█i M█rsh too."

Nobody answered. It was past one in the morning, Day Twenty-Three already gone over into Twenty-Four. Bram was asleep across the doorway again, a long dark shape with his shield propped against the frame. Nora was in the other bed with her bad arm on a pillow. The only light in the room came from the window still floating over my knees, the color of old paper.

**[Form: Bearer.]**
**[Exercise requirements: Same name. Active private account. Present at the door.]**

It was printed smaller than the lines above it, the way the important parts always are.

Bearer.

In the old life, in Year Four, a guild in the Market District had issued bearer notes for a month, before the Exchange banned them. A bearer note didn't belong to a name. It belonged to whoever was holding it. You could lose one in a card game. You could find one in a dead man's coat. Whoever stood at the counter with the paper in his hand could cash it.

The ledger had scratched letters out of the holder's name, and for twenty days I'd read that as a wound. Something lost. Somebody hiding.

Nobody was hiding. The name was a blank. Eli Marsh, with holes punched in it, so that any Eli Marsh could fill it.

Same name. I had it. I'd had it for twenty-five years in this body and thirty-two in my head.

Active private account. Loan Seven. Principal ten thousand four hundred and eighteen, as of noon. Four thousand due on Day Thirty-Three. Nobody in the city had a more active private account than I did.

Present at the door. The library. Day Thirty, twelve o'clock, zero minutes, zero seconds.

I got the notebook out from under my pillow, our cheap spiral ledger from the Market District stalls, and turned back to Day Nine, where I'd copied down the soot-letters from the Kiln wall in my own handwriting, which was also his handwriting.

*Day Thirty. I open the door from my side. Be the bearer. — E.*

"Be the bearer," I whispered. "You could've just said hold the paper."

"Who are you talking to?"

Nora hadn't moved. Her eyes were open in the dark.

"You're supposed to be asleep."

"You said your own name twice. With holes in it. I could hear the holes." She sat up carefully, keeping the arm still, and held out her good hand. "Give me the ledger. Joint ledger. Whatever you just found, it goes on the page."

---

So I sat on the end of her bed and told her. All of it. On Day Eleven she'd drawn a line down the middle of a page and written *E* on one side and *N* on the other, and since then there hadn't been a number she didn't see. Every night she checked my side. About once a week she found something, and she never let me forget it.

"You're off by one on the stall take, by the way," she said, before I'd finished. "Day Twenty-Two. Owen wrote forty-two, you wrote forty-one."

"Nora."

"I'm listening. I can listen and be right at the same time."

"Vesper told me on Day Sixteen," I said. "When a senior lienholder forecloses, everything underneath is wiped out. Every junior claim. Her firm's. The Lender's."

"You told me. You said she said it like she was reading a weather report."

"So this is the plan." I took the pencil out of the spiral and turned to a clean page, and wrote it as I said it, the way I'd lay out a trade.

*Day 30, 12:00:00. Hale Memorial Library, front door.*
*1. Eli exercises the senior lien as bearer.*
*2. All junior claims on Nora extinguished:*
*— Loan 6 (Ashgrave). Gone.*
*— Loan 7, lien on Nora (Lender). Gone.*
*3. Senior lien released.*
*4. Nora: on no ledger.*

She read it the way she read everything I wrote now, slowly, with her finger under the line, looking for the place where I'd lied.

"On no ledger," she said.

"Nobody's collateral. No gray cord. No Day Thirty."

"And your loan? Seven?"

"Still there. Ten thousand four hundred and eighteen, four thousand due on Thirty-Three." I shrugged. "But without you written on it, it's just a debt. I know how to pay debts. I've been doing it for twenty-three days."

She didn't say anything for a while. Down in the street, someone was dragging a cart over the paving stones, and the wheels made a sound like a coin rolling in a drawer.

"The other one," she said finally. "The one who wrote it. Silas said the name on the lien was him. The sixth one."

"Yes."

"If you use his paper. If the lien gets released." She tapped the word *released*. "Then what happens to him?"

I'd been waiting for that question for about forty minutes. I'd known it was coming since she sat up. I still didn't have anything good.

"I don't know," I said.

"Eli."

"I don't. That's the true answer. He's a line in a margin. He's soot on a wall. I don't know if there's anything left of him to happen to." I looked at the page. "He wrote *I open the door from my side.* So I think he's on the other side of something. That's all I've got."

Nora took the pencil back. Under step four, she wrote, in her own fast, round handwriting:

*5. Find out what happens to him.*

Then she underlined it twice, which was Owen's habit, and she'd caught it from him the way you catch a cold.

"He's an idiot," she said. "You can tell from the handwriting. Same as you."

"That's fair."

"But he's *our* idiot." She shut the ledger. "Lie down. You've got the stall at eight."

---

Clipboard House opened at eight, and by eight-fifteen the line went past the fountain.

Ten days ago it had been a folding table on the courthouse plaza, a hand-lettered sign, and Owen's clipboard. Now it was two tables, a canvas awning Maya had stitched out of Vanguard's old banner with the white V cut out of the middle, and a waiting list.

People brought us everything. Guild contracts. Loan offers from three new lending desks that had opened by the board, all of them with a clause somewhere that said something unpleasant in small type. Monster parts from Floor Two, gold-coin golem shavings, chips of scale-brass. A man came in with a ring he'd found in the Drowned Campus mud and wanted to know if it was magic.

**[Brass Ring — Market Value: 1 Merit | True Value: 1 Merit]**

"It's a ring," I told him.

"Is it worth anything?"

"One Merit. Both ways. That's rarer than you'd think." I handed it back. "Somebody loved it once, probably. That's not on the window."

Owen wrote it down. Owen wrote everything down. *Ring, appraised, 1/1, no fee charged.* Two percent of one Merit was a fiftieth of a coin, and he'd decided on Day Fifteen that we didn't charge fractions to people holding the only nice thing they owned.

The other change was the fifteen.

When I voided their member agreements on Floor Two, the last fifteen members of Vanguard had walked out of the Counting House with nothing. No guild, no contracts, no armbands. Gear that still technically belonged to the charter, which meant it belonged to me, which I'd given back to them on the morning of Day Twenty-One, piece by piece, because I didn't want to own fifteen people's armor.

On Day Twenty-One, four of them had shown up at the stall to get their new contracts read. Other guilds had already come looking for them.

On Day Twenty-Two, six had come, and stayed.

By Day Twenty-Four they'd organized themselves without anyone asking them to. Three stood at the edge of the plaza in shifts, watching the line. Two walked customers with large sales to the board and back. One of them, a heavy, quiet Warrior who'd been Damon's second shield, sat on an upturned crate behind me all morning and didn't say anything until a scavenger tried to cut the line with a sack of golem shavings.

"Back," the Warrior said.

The scavenger looked at him. He went to the back.

"You don't have to do this," I said to the Warrior, later, when the line thinned. "None of you work for me. There's no guild. I made sure of it."

"We know." He turned a gold-coin golem chip over in his fingers. "You read my contract for free, on the courthouse steps. Day Six. Clause Two." He put the chip down. "I was one of the fifteen who stayed. Even after you read it. I stayed anyway."

"Why?"

"Because he was paying." He shrugged. "Here, nobody's paying me, and I still eat. Owen splits whatever's left over from the fee pool at night. Everybody gets the same." He looked across the plaza at the board. "Equal shares. Never had that before."

At noon, I closed the window on the day's split and did the arithmetic out loud, because I always did.

"Gross fees, three hundred and eight. Guards' pool, sixty. Supplies, nine. Two hundred and thirty-nine left, five ways. Forty-seven and four left over. Three go to the pool." I looked at Owen. "The odd Merit's Nora's this time. Yesterday was mine."

"I know," said Owen, who had it written down already.

Nora came by at half past twelve with two paper cups of the bitter barley tea they sold by the board, and set one down on my side of the table without asking. She leaned over Owen's shoulder and read the split.

"Forty-eight for me," she said. "Write that I got the odd one."

"It's written."

"Write it bigger."

He did, too. Then he drew a small square beside her name and ticked it, which was how Owen said *paid*, and she looked at the tick for a second longer than she needed to. Eleven days ago she hadn't known what was written about her in anyone's book. Now she checked the ticks.

Then the afternoon book. Owen and I read contracts after lunch, two Merit a page, split down the middle between the two of us because nobody else could read them. That came to twelve for me.

"Forty-seven and twelve," I said. "Fifty-nine."

**[Balance: 9 + 59 = 68 Merit]**

Sixty-eight Merit, six days before the door. On Day Three I'd have called that a fortune. On Day Twenty-Four it was just a number that was going up.

---

I told the others that afternoon, at the back table, while Maya stitched a strap on Bram's shield and the Warrior stood with his back to us, pretending he couldn't hear.

Jonah read the plan off the ledger and then sat back.

"So we walk to the library on Day Thirty," he said. "You say a magic sentence. Everything goes away."

"It's not magic. It's priority. The oldest claim wins."

"Does the Lender know?"

"He knows it's there. Yesterday he called it inconvenient. Bad for business."

"Then he knows," said Jonah, and went back to waxing his bowstring.

Owen had turned his clipboard over and was writing on the back, under the four names of the shareholders' register. *DAY 30 — 12:00:00 — LIBRARY.* He boxed it. Then he looked up.

"Ashgrave holds Six," he said. "Ashgrave collects on Day Thirty at noon. You exercise on Day Thirty at noon. What happens if they get there first?"

"They can't get there first. It's the same second."

"What if it isn't?" He tapped the box with his pen. "Your whole plan is that it's still Day Thirty when they come. Nobody's promised you that."

Bram looked up from the shield. Nora looked at me.

"Jonah," I said. "On Floor Two. On the stairs, after. What did Damon say to Vesper?"

Jonah stopped waxing.

"*The collateral used Hold the Line,*" he said. "*Impairment's on the record, yes?*"

I'd written that down too, on Day Twenty. It was on my side of the ledger, in a box of its own, with a question mark next to it that I hadn't known how to answer.

"Impairment," said Owen. "As in damage."

"As in damage to the collateral."

Nobody said anything. Bram set the shield down.

"Then what does she get?" Bram said quietly. "If they say she's damaged."

"I don't know yet," I said, which I was getting tired of saying.

---

I didn't sleep much. Nobody in the hotel did.

It had been Vanguard's headquarters until Day Twenty, and the lease had come to me with the rest of the charter's assets: a lobby, fifteen empty rooms, and a bill of forty Merit a week. On Day Twenty-Four I'd let the six who stayed at the stall take rooms on the second floor, since I was paying for the rooms either way. By that night the corridor sounded like a dormitory. Doors, boots, someone laughing at the far end. Bram still slept across our doorway. Nobody had asked him to.

On the morning of Day Twenty-Five, Nora came down to the stall with her hair still wet and stood in front of the table.

"Do it," she said.

"Do what?"

"Appraise me. You do it every morning. You think I don't notice. You do it when you think I'm looking the other way." She put her good hand on her hip. "Do it where I can see you. Joint ledger."

So I did.

**[Appraise]**

**[Nora Marsh — Human — Warden (Lv. 17)]**
**[Market Value: — Not for sale.]**
**[True Value: — Not for sale.]**
**[Status: Pledged as Collateral.]**
**[Lienholder (Current): Private Lender. Account: Eli Marsh.]**
**[Lienholder (Prior): E█i M█rsh. Form: Bearer.]**
**[Message pending (1): Door — Hale Memorial Library. Opens: Day 30, 12:00:00.]**

Everything was where it had been yesterday. I let out a breath.

Then the bottom of the window flickered, the way the board flickers when a price changes during a trade, and a new line wrote itself underneath, in the gray of Ashgrave's paper, which I'd only ever seen on Vesper's cuffs.

**[Loan 6 (Holder: Ashgrave Recoveries) — Collection Schedule Amended.]**
**[Collection: Day 30 → Day 28, 12:00]**
**[Basis: Acceleration — Impairment of Collateral.]**

I read it three times. The numbers didn't change.

"What," said Nora. "Eli. Read it out."

"Day Twenty-Eight," I said. "Noon."

"That's—"

"Three days." I did it out loud because my mouth was going to say something and I wanted it to be arithmetic. "Today's Twenty-Five. Twenty-Eight at noon is three days from now. The door opens on Thirty."

Owen had stopped writing. The Warrior behind me had turned around.

Day Twenty-Eight at noon, Ashgrave would come with its gray cords.

Day Thirty at noon, the door would open.

Nora put her hand flat on the table next to mine, because mine was shaking, and pressed until it stopped. She didn't say anything. She was reading the window over my shoulder with her finger in the air under the line, the way she read the ledger, looking for the place where it lied.

It didn't lie. Paper never did. That was the whole problem with it.

In between, there were two days, forty-eight hours exactly, and I had nothing to fill them with.
