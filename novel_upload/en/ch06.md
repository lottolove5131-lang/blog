# Chapter 6: Installment

Nora found me on the library steps an hour later, still holding the page.

The lawn below us was mud from end to end, steaming a little under the new blue sky. Owen was out there with his clipboard, supervising the recovery of the pillowcase like it was a crime scene. Jonah and two of the Warriors were poking the Hollow Matron's body with a broken oar to make sure it was dead. It was very dead. It was also, according to my skill, worth two thousand Merit in parts, and none of us could lift even one of them.

Nora sat down next to me. She didn't say anything for a while. She didn't need to.

"You said *later*," she said eventually. "It's later."

I folded the page and put it inside my coat. "How much do you want to know?"

"All of it."

"All of it's a lot."

"Then start with the part where you knew about the water."

So I told her.

Not all of it. I want to be honest about that, too. I told her about the Exchange buying the world, and the seven years, and the hundredth floor, and Damon. I told her about the closet and the canned peaches, and I made myself say the next part out loud, which was that the last time around, she hadn't come out of Zone Nine. I told her about the man with no face and the chair and the pen. I told her I owed him twelve thousand Merit, and that the first payment was due at noon on Day Three.

I didn't tell her what I'd put up as collateral.

I'd meant to. I opened my mouth to do it three separate times. Every time, I saw her face on the fourth floor landing, looking out over the fire axe, and I couldn't make the words come out. *You're the thing he takes if I fail.* I didn't know how you said that to someone. I didn't know how you said it to her.

When I finished, she sat there with her arms around her knees, looking at the mud.

"Seven years," she said.

"Yes."

"And I was dead for all of it."

"Yes."

"Did you get married?"

I blinked. "What?"

"In seven years. Did you get married? Did you get a dog? Did you go anywhere nice?" She was looking at me now, and her eyes were wet, and she was furious about it. "You're telling me you lived seven whole years, and I want to know if *any of it* was good."

I thought about it honestly.

"There was a bakery," I said, "in Year Four. Near the guild hall. They made these little cinnamon things. I went every Sunday."

She stared at me.

"That's it? A *bakery?*"

"They were very good cinnamon things."

She laughed. It came out of her like a hiccup, surprised, and then she put her face in her knees and laughed properly, and then she was crying, and I put my arm around her shoulders and let her do both.

"You're so stupid," she said into her knees. "You're the stupidest person I know."

"I'm aware."

"How much do you still need? For the payment."

"Three hundred and ninety-two."

"By noon on Day Three."

"Yes."

She lifted her head and wiped her face on her sleeve and looked out at the mud, at the dead Matron, at Owen's clipboard, with an expression I'd seen on my mother's face every month when the bills came.

"Okay," she said. "Then let's go get it."

I went through the whole campus twice looking for things to appraise. The student union. The bookstore. The physics labs, where I found a lot of equipment that the Exchange valued at less than zero, which I hadn't known was possible. Owen's clipboard, which I appraised as a joke and which turned out to be worth four Merit, and which he refused to sell. Nora's fire axe.

**[Fire Axe (Standard) — Market Value: 11 Merit | True Value: 11 Merit]**

The others had spent what they'd earned or were holding on to it, and I didn't blame them. They didn't owe me anything. I'd already asked them to trust me once. When Jonah offered me his whole share without being asked, forty-one Merit, I took it, and I felt sick about it, and I wrote it down on the back of the ledger page so I wouldn't forget.

**[Balance: 614 Merit]**

Seventy-eight short.

**[First Installment: 692 Merit. Due: 00:52:30]**

At fifty minutes, Nora found me sitting on the roof of Harlan Hall, alone, with the ledger page in one hand and nothing in the other.

"You're short," she said.

"Seventy-eight."

"What happens if you don't pay?"

I didn't answer.

She sat down next to me. The roof was warm from the sun. A plain, clean afternoon. Below us, the campus looked almost normal, if you ignored the mud line on every wall and the two-thousand-Merit corpse on the front lawn.

"Eli," she said. "What happens?"

"He takes the collateral."

"What's the collateral?"

"Something I can't afford to lose."

She looked at me for a long time. I knew she was working it out. She'd always been faster than me at this kind of math.

Then the air next to me went dark.

---

"Mr. Marsh," said the Lender. "I thought I'd come in person. Most of my clients prefer it, at this stage."

He was sitting in his chair on the gravel roof, legs crossed, ledger on his knee, exactly as he'd been on the hundredth floor. Nora didn't react. She was looking at me, and she didn't move, and I realized that she wasn't moving at all. Nothing was. A pigeon on the edge of the roof had one wing half open.

"She can't see you," I said.

"No. This is between the two of us." The blank oval of his face tilted toward me. "Seventy-eight Merit. With forty-nine minutes left. I have to say, you've done considerably better than your predecessors at this point."

"My predecessors."

"You've read the page, I'm sure."

"They were all me."

"Were they?" He said it lightly. "That's a philosophical question, and I'm an accountant."

"Six of them defaulted. Five of them had their collateral *seized*, and I don't even know what they put up." I held up the page. "And number six put up her. *Collection pending.* What does that mean?"

"It means the debt was sold." He turned a page in his ledger. "I don't collect on everything personally, Mr. Marsh. It isn't efficient. When a loan goes bad, I sell the paper to someone who specializes in that kind of work, at a discount, and they collect when it's convenient for them. Loan Six is no longer mine. I'm afraid I can't tell you who holds it now. Client confidentiality."

"Somebody's going to come for her," I said. "Whatever I do."

"Somebody may. That's between you and them. My business with you is Loan Seven." He closed the ledger. "Which brings us back to seventy-eight Merit."

"I don't have anything left to sell."

"That isn't true," said the Lender. "You have quite a lot. You just haven't appraised it."

I stared at him.

Then, slowly, I understood, and something inside me went cold.

**[Appraise]**

I'd never tried it on myself this way. Not my body, not my condition. The things underneath. And the skill understood what I was asking, the way it always had, and it opened a long list in front of me in the old paper color.

**[Memory: Supply closet, Day Zero through Day Three (Loop 7, prior) — True Value: 2 Merit]**
**[Memory: Cinnamon rolls, Hollis Street Bakery, Year Four — True Value: 18 Merit]**
**[Memory: Nora, age one, first steps (kitchen, the old house) — True Value: 610 Merit]**
**[Memory: Father leaving — True Value: 0 Merit]**
**[Memory: Mother's face — True Value: 260 Merit]**

I read the list three times.

My whole life, priced. The worst three days of it were worth two Merit. Of course they were. Who would want them.

"Thirty-five percent of True Value," the Lender said gently. "The usual terms. I'll waive the twelve-hour limit, since I came all this way. You only need seventy-eight."

Nora's first steps would clear it seven times over. I remembered that day. I'd been six. She'd let go of the kitchen chair and walked four whole steps to me and fallen on her face and laughed, and Mom had screamed so loudly the neighbors came over.

I wasn't going to sell that.

Two hundred and sixty times thirty-five percent was ninety-one.

"Can I pick which parts?" I said. My voice sounded strange to me. "Of Mom. Can I keep her voice?"

"The listing is for her face," said the Lender. "Only her face."

I thought about Nora, sitting frozen next to me, with our mother's face on her own. I thought about the photo on Nora's phone that she'd shown me in the lounge on the first night, Mom at the lake, squinting into the sun. Nora had photos. Nora had her own memory. It wasn't like it would be gone from the world.

It would just be gone from me.

"Sell it," I said.

I didn't feel anything happen. That was the worst part. I'd expected it to hurt.

"Thank you, Mr. Marsh," said the Lender. "A pleasure, as always."

The pigeon finished opening its wing.

Nora was mid-breath. She finished it, and blinked, and frowned at me, because as far as she knew I'd been sitting there with nothing in my hands, and now I looked like I'd been punched.

"What just happened?"

"I paid it."

"With *what?* You said you were short seventy-eight."

"I found something."

"You found something. On a roof. In the four seconds I was looking at you." She leaned closer and looked at my face, the way she used to look at the thermometer when I was sick and she was little and wanted to be the one to read it. "You've gone white, Eli."

"I'm tired."

"You're lying."

"I'm also tired," I said. "Both things can be true."

She didn't believe me. I could see her not believing me. But she was twenty, and she'd just spent three days in a flooded dorm, and there was a strip of clean blue sky over the campus for the first time since noon on Saturday. After a moment she let it go, and leaned her shoulder against mine, and we sat there and watched Owen down on the lawn trying to get Jonah to stop poking the dead Matron.

"Whatever it was," she said quietly, "thank you."

I didn't answer, because I didn't know what I'd sold yet. Not really. It's hard to miss a thing when you can't remember what it looked like.

I found out that afternoon.

Nora wanted to go to the cemetery.

It was Tuesday, two days late. Nobody was driving, so we walked, the two of us, across a city that looked like somebody had shaken it and set it down again. The flowers she'd bought for Mom's birthday had survived on the second floor of Harlan Hall, in a coffee mug, a little dry at the edges. She carried them the whole way.

The cemetery was quiet. It had been quiet before the world ended too. I found the stone without thinking about it; my feet knew the way. Nora knelt and put the flowers down and brushed some leaves off the grass.

"Happy birthday," she said. "Sorry we're late. Eli jumped on a monster."

Then she took out her phone, even though there was no signal and hadn't been for three days, and opened a photo, and turned it so I could see.

"This is my favorite one," she said. "At the lake. Remember? You took it."

It was a woman on a dock, squinting into the sun, laughing at something off to the side. She had dark hair tied back badly and a faded blue sweatshirt.

I didn't know who she was.

I knew who she had to be. I knew it the way you know a fact from a textbook. I looked at her eyes and her mouth and the way she held her head, and I waited for the thing that should have happened, the thing that happens when you see your own mother's face, and nothing came. She looked kind. She looked like a stranger.

"Yeah," I said. "I remember."

Nora looked at me. She had our mother's face. I only knew that because people used to tell us.

The paper-colored window opened while she was still looking at me.
