# Chapter 16: Year Nine

I woke her and read the line out loud, because that was the rule now. Every number in the open. Every line she could see.

"Door. Hale Memorial Library. Opens Day Thirty, twelve o'clock, zero seconds."

Nora was sitting cross-legged on the other bed with our ledger open on her knees. It was the cheap spiral notebook from the Market District stalls, and it had two handwritings in it now: mine, which leaned right, and hers, which stood up straight and pressed so hard it came through to the next page. She wrote the line down exactly as I read it. Then she looked up.

"The library," she said. "Our library. With the door."

"Yes."

"The door where somebody wrote *don't let her hold the line.*"

"Yes."

She looked at the notebook for a long time. The hotel's heating clicked on and ticked in the wall. Somewhere down the hall, Jonah was snoring through a door that didn't shut properly.

Then she closed the ledger, put the pen in the spiral, and stood up.

"Where are you going?"

"Down the hall," she said. "Silas told us a story with the end cut off. I want the end."

---

Silas Rook had the room at the end of our corridor, the one with the broken ice machine outside it. Bram had asked for the room next to his. Nobody had asked Bram why, and he hadn't said.

Nora didn't knock. The door wasn't locked. Silas was sitting at the little desk under the window with a bottle of something brown and a single paper cup, and he didn't look surprised to see her. He looked surprised to see her carrying the fire axe.

"Miss Marsh," he said.

"Year Nine," Nora said. "Day Three."

Bram's door opened behind us. He filled the hallway in a T-shirt and bare feet and didn't say anything, just leaned on the frame, the way he leaned on his shield between discharges.

"I told you," Silas said. "I left before that."

Nora put the axe down on his desk. Not hard. She laid it flat, beside the bottle, with the blade toward him, the way you'd put a contract on a table.

"You knew the sigil timings in the Kiln better than Eli did," she said. "You knew which alcove had the wall. You knew my nickname in a world where I'm dead in a dorm. People who leave early don't know that much." She sat down on the end of his bed. "I'm the collateral. I'm the thing on the door. I'm the one who gets taken on Day Thirty. So you're going to tell me what happened on Year Nine, Day Three, or I'm going to sit here until you do. I've got fifteen days."

Silas looked at her. Then at me. Then at the axe.

"You really are her," he said quietly.

He poured a second cup and pushed it across the desk. Nora didn't touch it.

---

"In that world," Silas said, "your brother ran the Marsh Exchange out of the old guild hall on Hollis Street. Next to a bakery. He priced everything for everybody, and he did it honestly, and he made the whole city rich enough to get past Year Seven. You know that part."

"Tell it anyway," Nora said.

"By Year Nine the Exchange was sending expeditions. Real ones, eighty people, supply lines. They went where nobody else could afford to go. In the spring of Year Nine they reached the eighty-eighth floor." He turned the paper cup in his fingers. "The boss on eighty-eight was called the Auditor. A big thing in a robe made of receipts. Two lenses where a face should be. It didn't care who hit it. It read the room, the way you two read things, and it went for whoever owed the most."

I did the arithmetic before I wanted to. A man in his ninth year, carrying a loan at one and a half percent a day, compounding, missing nothing, paying every installment on time.

"That was him," I said.

"That was Marsh. Whatever he owed, it was more than eighty people put together." Silas didn't look at me. "So it came for him. And you—" he nodded at Nora "—they called you the Wall of Sixty. You held the sixtieth floor alone for eleven minutes in Year Five. Every Warden in the city wanted to be you. When the Auditor turned toward your brother, you used Hold the Line."

Nora's hands were flat on her knees.

"How long?" she said.

"Ten minutes."

"Did it work?"

"It worked. The expedition got the Auditor's lenses cracked and the whole party out to the stairs. You held for ten minutes and four seconds. They timed it. People talked about the four seconds for a year." He drank. "You didn't come down."

Nobody said anything. Down the hall, Jonah's snoring stopped and started again.

"Noon that day," Silas said, "was an installment. Loan Six. I didn't know that then. Everybody knows it now. He'd been carrying it for nine years and never missed a day. And that morning he walked out of the stairwell with you over his shoulder and he spent everything. The Exchange's treasury. Recovery draughts by the crate. When those didn't work, there was a rite at the temple on Ninth Avenue that the board sold for forty thousand Merit. Three percent success rate. He bought it twice."

"Twice," I said.

"Six percent, he said. Better than three." Silas almost smiled. "That was the only joke anybody heard him make that day. Noon came while the second rite was still burning. He missed the installment. He didn't even look at the window."

I knew what that window looked like. I'd seen its color on a gravel roof and a hotel nightstand and the hundredth floor. Old paper. Handwritten.

"And then?" said Nora.

"And then that night, around eleven, he put on his coat. He told the people at the hall he was going to go sit in the chair. That's what he said. *I'm going to go sit in the chair.* He walked out alone." Silas set the cup down. "Nobody saw him again. The next week the Exchange had a new name over the door, and by the end of the month people had started saying he'd never been real. That he was a story. You'd be surprised how fast a city forgets a man who saved it."

"I wouldn't," I said.

---

I'd been carrying it around for fourteen days, the carving on the library door.

*DON'T LET HER HOLD THE LINE.*

I had read it the way I read everything. As a warning. A clause. Information from someone who knew what was inside the building. And it had been that. Nora had used Hold the Line in the library, and the Drowned Librarian had nearly put her in the ground, and I'd thought, all right, he was right about the library.

It hadn't been about the library at all.

It had been about ten minutes and four seconds on the eighty-eighth floor, and a man with his sister over his shoulder, and two rites at three percent. He'd sent it back through the only door he had. He'd scratched it into a water stain with a knife, and the letters leaned hard to the right because his hand had been shaking, and the *t*s were crossed too high because that was how we'd always crossed them.

*Recorded: Year Nine, Day Three.*

The lien. The same day.

He'd lost her at ten in the morning, missed the payment at noon, and by midnight he'd put a claim on the next one of her, senior to everything, so that nobody else could take her first. Not the Lender. Not whoever bought the paper.

Not me.

"He wasn't telling me about a boss," I said. My voice came out flat. "He was telling me about you."

Nora stood up.

She picked the axe up off Silas's desk. She didn't cry. I'd been braced for it, the way I'd been braced on the library steps, the way I'd been braced every time she found out a new piece of what I'd done with her. Her eyes were dry and very bright.

"That guy's an idiot," she said. "Same as you."

"Nora—"

"Forty thousand Merit. Twice. For three percent." She shook her head once. "And then he walks out at eleven at night and doesn't tell anybody where he's going. That's you. That's exactly you. You'd have done all of it."

I didn't argue. She was right, and we both knew it, and arguing would only have proved it.

She walked past Bram in the doorway. Bram turned sideways to let her through, which for Bram was like a building stepping off the sidewalk.

"Where are you going?" I said.

"Roof," she said. "Alone. Don't follow me."

I took one step. Bram put his hand flat on my chest. It was like being stopped by a door.

"She said alone," he said.

"She's my sister."

"I know. That's why she said it to you." He didn't move his hand. "I held the line fourteen times for the Pact, Eli. The worst part was never the hit. It was the people behind me watching. Let her do it without anyone watching."

---

I went back to my room and didn't sleep.

I sat on the bed with the ledger and did the only thing I knew how to do with a feeling, which was turn it into numbers.

Day Fifteen, a few minutes before midnight. Fifteen days to the library door. Eight days to the third installment. Three thousand five hundred Merit due at noon on Day Twenty-Three. Seven hundred and ninety-five in my account. The House's take sitting in Owen's column on the back of his clipboard, where Owen insisted it stay until we needed it.

Two thousand seven hundred and five short, before the House.

Then I did the other arithmetic. The one nobody had asked for.

A man pays every installment on time for nine years. Day Three, Day Thirteen, every one after it. I knew roughly how big they got. I'd seen the schedule. By Year Nine, with the compounding, he'd have been paying more each week than the whole city had owned in Year One. And he'd done it. He'd never missed. That was the part that kept going around in my head. Nine years of never missing, and he'd missed it on the one day it was the only thing he had left to lose.

Except it hadn't been. He'd still had her paper. He'd still had a claim.

Ten minutes and four seconds.

I opened Nora's window again. I didn't need to. I knew every line by now. I did it anyway, the way you touch a bruise.

**[Nora Marsh — Human — Warden (Lv. 13)]**
**[Status: Pledged as Collateral.]**
**[Lienholder (Prior): E█i M█rsh.]**
**[Recorded: Year 9, Day 3.]**
**[Door: Hale Memorial Library. Opens: Day 30, 12:00:00.]**

It had looked like a threat for fifteen days. Now it looked like a man holding a door shut with his whole body.

---

She came down at dawn.

I was already at the courthouse plaza, because the House opened at seven and Owen opened it whether or not anyone else showed up. Our stall was two folding tables we'd bought off a dead noodle vendor's cousin, a tarp, and a hand-painted board that Maya had lettered for us: **CLIPBOARD HOUSE — FAIR APPRAISAL — 2%**. Owen had added, underneath, in smaller marker, *we read the fine print.*

Nora walked up with her hood up and her eyes red at the edges, and sat down on the crate behind the second table, and held out her hand for the ledger.

I gave it to her.

She uncapped the pen and wrote, at the top of the Day Sixteen column, in her upright pressed-hard letters:

*Hold the Line: 0 uses.*

Then she passed it back.

"Every day," she said. "I'm going to write it every day. So you don't have to ask."

"Nora."

"Ten minutes is a long time." She looked out at the plaza, at the first customers drifting toward the stall with their contracts folded in their fists. "I'd have done it too. That's what I was thinking about up there. Not that he was stupid. That I'd have done it too, without even deciding."

"I know," I said. "That's the problem."

"Yeah." She pulled her hood down. "So I'm writing it down."

Owen, who had been pretending not to listen, slid a cup of instant coffee in front of her and went back to his clipboard.

---

The day was ordinary, which helped.

A Ranger from Zone Six brought us a guild contract with an exit fee buried in an appendix; Owen found it before I did and looked so pleased with himself that I let him read it aloud. Two students tried to sell us a sack of Silt Leech cores at five each, and I told them the board would pay eight, and they looked at me like I'd grown a second head and then went to the board and came back an hour later with a bag of bread for the stall. Jonah sat on an upturned bucket at the end of the tables with his bow across his knees and glared at anyone who raised their voice. Bram stood behind us like a wall and made the line very orderly without saying a word.

Silas didn't come down. Nobody went up to get him.

By late afternoon the House had done thirty-one appraisals, and Owen had written every one on the clipboard, and at the bottom he'd drawn a line and written the fee total and then, underneath, *no complaints*, and underlined it twice.

At one point I looked up and caught Nora reading her own appraisal. She'd figured out how to ask me for it without asking. She just held out her hand, palm up, and waited, and I opened it, and she read the door line and gave it back. She did it three times that day.

The third time she said, "Thirteen days and twenty hours."

"Roughly."

"You'll have it to the second by tonight."

"I already have it to the second."

She almost smiled. "Of course you do."

---

The plaza emptied out after sunset the way it always did. The board's gold listings brightened as the sky went dark, and the stalls along the courthouse steps lit their lanterns, and the bread sellers packed up. Owen was counting the fee jar into his column. Jonah had gone to get food. Nora was folding the tarp with Bram, both of them pulling a corner, arguing about whether the tarp was worth repairing.

I was writing up the last appraisal when the light on the table changed.

Someone was standing in front of it, between me and the lanterns.

Long gray coat. Short pale hair. Hands empty. The calm, card-player's face I'd seen at the edge of this same plaza on Day Six, and at the foot of the Spire on Day Seven, and on the message wall beside Damon three days ago.

Vesper Kaine looked down at Owen's clipboard, read the top line upside down, and then looked at me.

"Mr. Marsh," she said. "Silas Rook lied to you about one thing. Who it was, in Year Nine, that walked your sixth self to the chair."
