# Chapter 30: Paid in Full

Day Thirty-One was a Tuesday.

I didn't notice until we were halfway to the cemetery, and then I couldn't stop noticing. The last time Nora and I had walked this road together, it had been a Tuesday too. Day Three. Two days late for Mom's birthday, with a coffee mug of flowers and a stranger's face on a phone.

This time Nora had bought the flowers from a stall at the edge of the courthouse square, from an Artisan who'd figured out how to grow chrysanthemums in a window box that the Exchange valued at four Merit. Yellow ones. She carried them the same way, in both hands, the stems wrapped in a page torn out of somebody's notebook.

"You're counting something," she said.

"Days."

"Stop it. It's a day off."

The city looked different from the first walk. Not fixed. Nothing was fixed. But people had swept the glass off the sidewalks, and somebody had painted prices on a bus shelter in white house paint, *WATER 1 / BREAD 2 / LEECH CORES BOUGHT AT 6 — FAIR PRICE — ASK FOR OWEN*, and there was a line of kids sitting on a wall outside a school, eating something out of paper cones. Normal. Stupid, ordinary, Tuesday normal.

My feet still knew the way.

---

The stone was where it had always been. Nora knelt and swept the leaves off the grass with the side of her hand and laid the chrysanthemums down, and then she sat back on her heels.

"Hi, Mom," she said. "We're on time. Well. We're thirty days late for your birthday. But we're on time for us."

She took her phone out of her hoodie pocket and opened the photo. The one at the lake. She didn't turn it toward me right away. She looked at it herself for a while first.

Then she held it out.

A woman on a dock, squinting into the sun, laughing at something off to the side. Dark hair tied back badly. A faded blue sweatshirt.

I looked at her for a long time. I gave it everything I had. Her eyes, her mouth, the angle of her head, the way her hand was half raised as if she was about to wave somebody off.

Nothing came.

I'd known it wouldn't. I'd still hoped, a little, that closing the account might have closed everything that came with it. But the Lender didn't do refunds. A sale was a sale, and I'd sold her on a roof on Day Three for ninety-one Merit, to cover a seventy-eight Merit gap, and the thirteen I'd had left over were long gone.

"I don't know her," I said.

Nora didn't lower the phone.

"I sold it," I said. "Her face. On the roof of Harlan Hall, when I was seventy-eight short. That was the thing I found. I should have told you a long time ago."

"I know."

"You know."

"I worked it out on Day Three. Right here." She lowered the phone, finally. "You said *I remember* like you were reading it off a card. You never once in your life remembered something without making it into a whole story. You'd have told me what she was laughing at."

I didn't have anything to say to that.

"Okay," Nora said. She stood up, brushed the grass off her knees, and faced me. Then she scowled. Not a real scowl. A deliberate one, eyebrows down, mouth tight, chin up. The face she made at Damon on the courthouse steps. The face she made at the man in the doorway yesterday.

"Then look at me," she said. "When I'm mad. Aunt Pam says I've got her face when I'm mad. So when you want to see her, make me mad. You're good at it."

I laughed.

I didn't mean to. It came out of me the way hers had on the library steps on Day Three, like a hiccup, surprised. And then I couldn't stop, and she was still holding the scowl, which made it worse, and after a while she gave up and laughed too, and we stood in front of our mother's stone like two idiots on the thirty-first day of the end of the world.

It wasn't her face. I'd never have her face. But it was the closest thing to it in the world, and it was alive, and it was annoyed at me.

That would do.

We stood there a while after that. A man two rows over was raking leaves off a grave with a broken hockey stick. The chrysanthemums leaned in the wind.

"Do you think he went somewhere?" Nora said. "Him. Yesterday."

She didn't say his name. Neither of us had a name for him that wasn't mine.

"I don't know," I said. That was the honest answer, and I'd promised her honest answers. "I know what the window said. *Removed.* I know he wanted it."

"That's not the same thing. He said."

"No."

She thought about that. Then she bent down and moved one of the chrysanthemums a few centimeters to the left, out of the row, so it lay a little apart from the others.

"That one's his," she said. "He's late too. He can have one."

---

On the way back, Nora took a folded sheet of paper out of her pocket and handed it to me without a word.

It was a page off the back of Owen's clipboard pad. She'd done the math in pencil, all of it, line by line, the way I'd shown her on the hotel floor on Day Six. *250 × 1.015*, and then the same again, and again, down the page and onto the back, twenty-seven lines, one for every day from Day Four. At the bottom, circled twice:

*373.7 → 374. Rounded UP. Your rules.*

"You compounded it by hand," I said.

"You said one and a half percent was the going rate."

"I said it was *his* going rate."

"And I lent you money anyway, so it's mine too." She held out her hand, palm up. "Three hundred and seventy-four."

I paid her. It was the first payment I'd made in thirty-one days that didn't come with a window in the color of old paper.

**[Transfer to Nora Marsh: 374 Merit.]**
**[Balance: 4,545 Merit]**

"Four thousand nine hundred and nineteen," I said, out of habit, "minus three seventy-four. Four thousand five hundred and forty-five."

"And forty dollars."

I stopped walking. "Nora."

"From Christmas."

"There aren't any dollars. There haven't been any dollars for a month. There isn't a bank in the city that—"

"Then I guess you're in default." She kept walking. "What happens when people default, Eli?"

I caught up with her at the corner. And then I saw where she was taking me.

It was a narrow brick shopfront on Hollis Street, just around the corner from the hotel that had been Vanguard's guild hall and was now, on paper, mine. Somebody had propped the door open with a brick. The window had a hand-lettered sign taped inside it. *OPEN. MERIT ONLY. NO CREDIT.* And the whole block smelled like butter, sugar, and the brown edge of something burning just a little.

I knew the smell. I hadn't sold that one.

In the old life, this place had opened in Year Four. Same baker, a short woman with flour up to her elbows. Same counter. I'd come every Sunday for three years, with a ledger under my arm, and eaten one at the window and pretended it was a normal week.

In this life, she'd opened on Day Twenty-Six. Three years early. I didn't know why. Maybe some of the old timeline had shifted when I laughed at Damon on Day Zero, like everything else.

"Six," I said to the baker. "The cinnamon ones."

**[Cinnamon Twist (x6) — Market Value: 2 Merit each.]**

One number. Twelve Merit for the bag. I didn't know what they were really worth. I'd never know again.

I handed Nora the bag.

"Interest," I said. "On the forty dollars. Principal's still outstanding."

She looked at the bag. Then at me. Then she took one out and bit into it, standing in the doorway, and chewed, and her face did something complicated.

"These are the cinnamon things," she said, with her mouth full.

"Year Four. Every Sunday."

"It's Tuesday."

"I'm making an exception."

She ate the rest of it in three bites. Then she took a second one out and handed it to me, and I ate it at the window, the way I used to, and it tasted exactly the way I remembered. Seven years of the old life, and that was the one thing I'd kept that tasted the same in this one.

"Okay," Nora said, wiping her fingers on her hoodie. "That's a good thing. From the seven years. I'm glad it was this."

---

The Clipboard House had moved off the courthouse square that morning, for good, because the line at the stall had got too long to fit between the board and the bread sellers. It lived in the lobby of the old Merchant Row Hotel now. Owen had insisted on keeping the folding table from the square anyway. He said customers trusted a folding table.

He was sitting behind it when we came in, with his clipboard and a new pen.

"Owen," I said. "I'd like to make a capital contribution."

He looked up at me. Then at Nora, who was holding the bag of cinnamon things behind her back, badly.

"How much?"

"Everything. Four thousand five hundred and forty-five."

Owen didn't say anything. He turned the clipboard over to the back, where the shareholder register lived, in his small, square handwriting. *Nora Marsh, 110. Jonah Webb, 110. Owen Park, 110. Bram Okafor, 110. Eli Marsh (founding).* Under that he wrote *+4,545, Day 31*, and the time, and then he held out the pen so I could initial it. He'd always made me initial things.

"You're still the appraiser," he said. "I'm still keeping the books."

"I can only see one number now."

"That's one more than the customers can usually see." He tapped the table. "There's a kid over there wants to sell a leech core for three."

I looked. A kid of maybe fifteen, holding a gray core out in his palm like it might bite him.

**[Silt Leech Core — Market Value: 6 Merit]**

"Six," I said to him. "Don't take less."

That was all. That was the job.

Across the lobby, Bram was showing the woman with the burn scar how the front door locked, and twenty-one other people from the Cinder Pact were arguing about who would take night watch. Silas was in the corner with a stack of paper, writing something long by hand, and not looking up. Jonah was asleep on a lobby couch with his bow across his chest. Maya had a workbench by the window, where she was putting new straps on a shield for Nora, and she waved a screwdriver at me without turning around.

The fifteen from the old Vanguard had painted over the white V on the front doors. Somebody had painted a clipboard there instead. It wasn't a very good clipboard.

---

**YEAR SEVEN**

The first thing I appraised on the hundredth floor, the second time, was myself.

Out of habit. Out of curiosity. I stood in the center of the room, on my own two legs, next to a pale stone disc covered in lines like the veins of a leaf, and I looked at my own price.

**[Eli Marsh — Human — Appraiser (Lv. 44)]**
**[Market Value: 3 Merit]**

Three.

Still three. Seven years, forty-four levels, and the open market had not changed its mind about me by a single Merit. I laughed out loud, alone in the biggest room in the world, and the sound went up and up and didn't come back down, because there wasn't a ceiling.

There was no slab on my legs. In my coat pocket, where an expired protein bar had been the last time, there was a cinnamon twist in a paper napkin. Nora had put it there at dawn, in case I got hungry somewhere stupid.

I appraised it. Old habit.

**[Cinnamon Twist — Market Value: 2 Merit]**

The same price as the protein bar. One Merit less than me.

Where the ceiling should have been, there was sky, and the sky was a ledger.

Columns of gold text a mile tall, scrolling slowly over the clouds, where every person on Earth could read them. The expedition had cleared this floor four days ago, properly, a hundred and twelve people in eight rotations with nobody left behind as bait. The Sovereign had come apart under them like a cheap watch. Its Seat sat empty in the middle of the floor.

**[Sovereign's Seat — Market Value: N/A]**
**[Note: Unoccupied.]**

Above it, the sky turned a page.

**[GLOBAL NOTICE]**
**[Acquisition Review: Earth (Asset #88,412-C)]**
**[Result: Performing.]**
**[Scheduled Liquidation: CANCELLED.]**
**[Next Review: Year 14.]**

Somewhere far below me, through a hundred floors of white stone, I could hear it start. A city noticing. Then another one. A sound like the whole planet letting out a breath it had been holding since a Saturday seven years ago.

"Eli!"

Nora's voice, from the stairwell. She'd been at the front of the expedition for four days, standing beside Bram, two shields where there used to be one, and she'd never once held the line alone.

"Eli, are you pricing yourself again? Get down here! Owen says if you're late for the photo he's docking your share!"

"Coming."

I put my hands in my coat pockets to go.

There was a card in the left one.

I hadn't put it there. It was heavy stock, the color of old paper, a little soft at the corners. The handwriting on it was small and upright and very even, the hand of a man who had written in ledgers for longer than my planet had had a name.

**[Account #7: Paid in Full.]**
**[— The Lender]**

And under it, in smaller letters, as if added as an afterthought:

**[Position closed. Thank you for your business.]**

I stood there and read it four times.

I thought about the other things the last seven years had held. The Clipboard House had eleven windows in four districts now, and still one folding table, in the lobby of the old hotel, because Owen said customers trusted a folding table. Silas had spent two years writing down everything he remembered of Year Nine and then burned it, page by page, on the roof, with Bram watching. The woman with the burn scar ran night watch. Jonah had married a Ranger who beat him at archery and never let him forget it. Nora had made Warden Lv. 61 and said she'd stop at sixty, and hadn't.

Until this card, I hadn't seen anything the color of old paper in seven years.

Seven years ago, on this floor, the sky had told everybody on Earth what they were worth, and everybody had paid it. In blood, in years, in faces they couldn't remember. We'd paid in installments. We'd paid late. Some of us had paid seven times.

I put the card back in my pocket and went down the stairs to my sister.

This time, the Exchange paid.
