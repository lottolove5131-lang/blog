# Chapter 26: Muster

I told Nora at breakfast.

All of it. The paper floor and the red lines in the sky, the scraped accounts, the kitchen chair. The man with my face and gray at his temples and a left hand black to the wrist. Floor eighty-eight. The noon he missed. The chair Silas walked him to.

She ate a whole protein bar while I talked, in small, careful bites, and didn't look up until I got to the part about the knock.

Then she put the wrapper down.

"He used my knock," she said.

"He said it was yours first."

"It *is* mine." She looked at the table for a long moment. "I made that up when I was six. It's the dumbest thing I ever made up."

"He said to tell you not to hold the line."

"Obviously he did." Her voice was flat. "Every version of you in the universe says that. You're like a parrot."

"And he said he was sorry about the bakery. In his loop it closed in Year Five. He never took you."

That one got through. I watched it land. She pressed her lips together and picked the wrapper back up and folded it into a very small square, and then into a smaller one, and then she couldn't fold it anymore.

"Did he look like you?" she said.

"Older. Tired. He had my smile. The one you hate."

"The sorry one."

"That one."

She nodded. Then she stood up, put on her boots, and said she was going to the stall to help Owen open, and she didn't say anything else about it all day.

At noon on Day Twenty-Eight, she checked her own status in the middle of the square, in front of the board, with a line of customers watching. I checked it with her. Nothing happened. The old acceleration date passed like any other minute. She let out a breath I hadn't known she was holding and went back to sorting leech cores into trays.

---

Day Twenty-Nine started at five in the morning on the back of Owen's clipboard.

He'd flipped it over and drawn the city on it in ballpoint, which took him most of a page, because Owen didn't believe in maps that weren't to scale. Merchant Row at the top. The courthouse square. The river channel road running south and east. Zone Nine at the bottom, by the bend in the old river, with three small squares for Harlan Hall, the boathouse, and the science building, and one bigger square for the library.

"Twenty-five minutes by car," I said. "On a good day. Before the Exchange."

"There are no cars," said Owen.

"Then four hours on foot, if we go the river road and nobody stops us. Three and a half if we push." I tapped the bottom of the page. "We leave at six tomorrow. Steps by ten. That gives us two hours of margin to deal with whatever's waiting."

"What's waiting?" said Jonah. He was sitting on an upturned crate with his bow across his knees, restringing it.

"Damon," I said. "Ashgrave hired someone to keep me away from that door at noon. Vesper warned me. It's him. And he'll have the Cinder Pact."

Bram was leaning against the wall of the stall with his arms folded. He didn't say anything. He didn't have to. Twenty-two of his old guildmates had walked out of the Spire with gray cords on their wrists on Day Seven, and not one of them had known his name.

"They'll be on the steps," Silas said. He was standing a little apart from the rest of us, the way he'd stood apart all month. "Collectors stand at the point of collection. It's in the procedure. I used to write the procedure."

"Then we go through them without hurting them."

"That's harder."

"I know."

---

Owen made a list. Of course he did. He wrote it under the map in neat block capitals, one name to a line, and read it out loud while the square filled up with the first stallholders of the morning.

Nora. Jonah. Owen. Bram. Silas Rook.

The fifteen from Vanguard. They'd been guarding the stall in shifts since the twenty-second, and since Day Twenty-Four they'd been sleeping in the old Merchant Row Hotel, the lease I'd taken off Damon, six of them at first and then all fifteen. Their foreman was a big Warrior with a shaved head who still flinched a little whenever I said the words *Line Holder*. He'd come over at dawn without being asked and said the fifteen would walk with us.

"You voided our contracts," he said. "You didn't have to. That's worth a walk."

Then Owen read out the customers.

That part of the list was longer than I expected. The student who'd almost sold a leech core for three Merit on the stall's first day, who'd come back every day since to tell me what he'd sold and for how much, the way kids show you a test. The skinny Artisan in the hardware-store apron, the one I'd cleaned out of Emberleaf Salve on Day Four, who'd figured out who I was a week later and made me pay him the difference out of spite and then started bringing me his contracts to read. A pair of sisters with a guild agreement that had a Line Holder clause buried on page three, which I'd found for them in about four seconds. Eleven more after that.

"They said they're coming," Owen said. "I didn't ask them. They asked me where to be."

"It's dangerous."

"I told them that." He tapped the clipboard. "One of them said, and I'm quoting, *he read my contract for free, I can walk for free.*"

I didn't know what to say to that, so I said nothing, and Owen wrote a small checkmark next to each name.

Maya came at nine with a handcart.

She'd turned into the best Artisan in the region somewhere between Day Fifteen and now, mostly because she'd been our supplier, and mostly because she never stopped working. The handcart had two shields in it. Bram's, the big tower shield he'd had since the Kiln, and a smaller one with a fresh coat of gray paint that I didn't recognize.

"I riveted plates across the face of Bram's," she said, "and lined the back. It'll take a lot more than it used to. And this one's Nora's."

Nora had been at the board. She came over, wiping her hands on her jeans, and stopped.

"I've got an axe," she said.

"You've been practicing with Bram's spare for two weeks," said Maya. "And you keep complaining it's too heavy. This one's for you. Same height as his. So the edges line up."

Nora picked it up. She held it out in front of her and looked at it, and then, without a word, she walked over to Bram and stood next to him and set her shield beside his. The edges lined up exactly.

"Two shields," Bram said.

"Two shields," said Nora.

---

I told her about the price at the river wall, after lunch.

The rest of them were packing. She'd gone down to the old flood wall below the square to sit and look at the water, which she did when she was thinking, and I sat next to her with my legs over the edge.

"I worked it out last night," I said. "What I can sell."

"You don't have anything."

"I have one thing." I opened my status and turned it toward her. You can't show anyone your status. But I'd learned on the courthouse steps that you could show them a notice, and I'd copied the line into one. "Appraiser. True Value forty-one thousand. The Lender buys at twenty percent."

She read it twice. She'd learned that from me.

"Eight thousand two hundred," she said.

"Plus four hundred and nineteen in my account by tonight. Eight thousand six hundred and nineteen."

"And you need eleven thousand five hundred and sixty-two." She did the subtraction faster than I did. She always had. "Two thousand nine hundred and forty-three short."

"Which is funny, because that was my balance on Day Six. After the salve."

"That's not funny, Eli."

"It's a little funny."

"How do you get the rest?"

"I don't pay it." I'd been thinking about this since 03:12 yesterday. "He told me himself, on Day Six, outside the Kiln. *I sell paper when the risk stops being worth the return.* An unsecured loan, called, to a man with eight thousand Merit and nothing else? That's paper nobody wants. I don't need to pay it off. I need to buy it."

"And if he won't sell?"

"Then I haggle." I shrugged. "It's worked on him before."

She was quiet for a while. A gull walked along the wall and looked at us, decided we didn't have any food, and walked away.

"If you sell it," she said. "The class. What's left?"

"I'm still an Appraiser. I'll still see prices. One number. Market value. Like everybody else who picked it." I looked at the water. "I just won't see the second number anymore. I won't see what things are really worth."

"So you'll be a normal appraiser."

"Yeah."

She thought about that.

"You'll be a normal appraiser," she said again, slowly, like she was tasting it, "and I'll be a normal Warden, and Jonah's a normal Warrior who shoots arrows for some reason, and Owen's a normal Cleric with a clipboard."

"That's the plan."

"Then you'll just be a normal brother."

I hadn't expected that. I don't know why. It was exactly the kind of thing she said.

"Just a normal brother," I said.

"That's fine. I'd take that." She bumped her shoulder against mine. Then she looked at the water again. "I won't hold the line tomorrow."

"Nora."

"I'm not saying it because he said to. Or you." She pulled her sleeves down over her hands. "I'm saying it because I've been thinking about him. Sitting in a margin for who knows how long because I said sorry and did it anyway. I'm not doing that to you. Not tomorrow."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I'll probably want to." She sighed. "Bram's going to stand next to me. That's the deal. Two shields."

Behind us, from the top of the wall, a voice like a slammed door said, "That's my job."

Bram was standing there with his new shield on his back and a bag of bread in one hand. He'd heard all of it, probably. He didn't look embarrassed.

"Holding the line," he said. "It's my job. It was my job before any of you showed up. She can stand next to me. She doesn't stand in front."

He held out the bag. It was from the bakery on Hollis Street, around the corner from the hotel. It had opened three days ago, years earlier than in my old life. Nora looked at it and then at me.

"Cinnamon things," Bram said. "Owen said."

---

The Lender found me at the stall at sunset.

I didn't see him arrive. I was counting the day's take, and I looked up and there was a chair on the far side of my counter, and him in it, with his long coat folded over his knees.

**[Balance: 419 Merit]**

"Four hundred and nineteen," he said. "Your stall did well this week."

"It's not mine. I've got four partners."

"So I hear. Very modern." He took a folded sheet of paper from inside his coat. It was the color of every window he'd ever opened in front of me. "I was passing. I thought you should have this before tomorrow. No fee."

I didn't take it. I appraised it on the counter.

**[Document: Loan #7 — Clause 4(b), Collateral Substitution (Certified Copy)]**
**[Text: In the event the substituted collateral is extinguished, released, or otherwise ceases to be available, collateral shall revert to the original term: Borrower's Existence. Lender may call the outstanding principal immediately upon reversion.]**
**[Market Value: 0 Merit | True Value: 0 Merit]**
**[Hidden Clauses: None.]**

Two windows in two days without a hidden clause. I didn't like the pattern.

"He told you," said the Lender. "In my margin. I'd rather you had it in writing. Verbal terms make for bad business."

"You're warning me."

"I'm informing you. It's not the same." His blank face turned toward the square, where Nora was helping Maya load the handcart. "I'll be at the door tomorrow, Mr. Marsh. In my professional capacity. Whatever happens at noon, I'll be the next creditor you speak to."

"Will you sell the note?"

He was quiet just long enough that I noticed.

"Ask me tomorrow," he said, and stood, and the chair was gone, and so was he.

I folded the copy in thirds and put it inside my coat, next to the place where the page from the Librarian's desk used to be.

---

Nobody slept much.

We'd taken over the lobby of the Merchant Row Hotel, all thirty-odd of us, with bedrolls on the marble and the shields stacked against the front desk. The fifteen took the watches. Silas sat by the door all night, awake, cleaning a dueling blade that didn't need cleaning. Jonah slept with his bow. Owen slept with his clipboard. Nora slept with her back against her new shield, her head tipped onto Bram's shoulder, which he allowed with the expression of a man being sat on by a cat.

I sat at the front desk under a single lamp and did the math one more time. Then once more.

A little before midnight I went over and stood next to Silas at the door.

"He asked me to pass something on," I said.

The cloth stopped moving on the blade.

"He said don't be hard on you. You thought you were being kind." I waited. "Then he changed his mind. He said be a little hard on you. You'd prefer it."

Silas looked at the blade for a long time. Then he laughed, without any sound, just his shoulders.

"I would," he said. "He was like that in Year Nine. The whole walk to the chair, he taught me how to compound interest. He corrected me when I got it wrong."

"Sounds like him."

"Sounds like you." He went back to the blade. "Tomorrow, Damon is mine. I'm the only one who knows what that job is."

At 23:58, I went outside to get some air.

The square was empty. The board stood in the middle of it, ten meters of gold glass, scrolling its listings in the dark the way it had every night since Day Four.

At 00:00, the scroll stopped.

Every listing on the board slid sideways and vanished. The whole face went blank gold. Then a single notice wrote itself across it, in letters two meters tall, so bright that the stones of the square threw shadows.

**[REGIONAL NOTICE — BOUNTY]**
**[Posted by: Ashgrave Recoveries]**
**[Object: Nora Marsh (Warden, Lv. 17). Collateral, Loan #6.]**
**[Task: Locate and deliver to agent of record, Hale Memorial Library, before Day 30, 12:00.]**
**[Reward: 1,000 Merit.]**

Somewhere on Merchant Row, a window lit up. Then another. Then a whole row of them, all down the street, as every person in the city who'd been awake to see the gold change color sat up in bed and looked at it.

A thousand Merit. More than most of them had earned in a month.

Behind me, the hotel door opened. Silas stood in it with his blade in one hand, reading the board over my head.

"They don't need her location," he said quietly. "The cords find collateral by ledger. They never needed a bounty."

"I know."

"Then what's it for?"

I looked down the street at the lighted windows. Somebody was already pulling on a coat.

"It's not for finding her," I said. "It's for putting a city between me and that door."
