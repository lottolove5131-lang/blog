# Chapter 11: The Sixth

We took him back to the hotel on Fifth Street because there was nowhere else to take him, and because Bram wouldn't leave him on the plaza, and wouldn't look at him either.

Silas Rook sat on the floor of our room with his back against the radiator and a bottle of water Owen had put in his hands. He hadn't opened it. The long sword lay across his knees in its scabbard. Up close, he looked older than mid-thirties, the way people look older when they haven't slept for a week, and there was a burn along the side of his neck that he hadn't let Owen touch.

Jonah sat on the edge of the bathtub with the door open and his bow across his lap. He didn't string an arrow. He didn't have to.

Bram stood at the window with his back to all of us.

Nora sat on the bed next to me, cross-legged, with her axe on the blanket where she could reach it.

"You said you knew the sixth," I said.

"I did." Silas turned the bottle over in his hands. "Not well. Nobody knew him well. But everybody knew him."

"Everybody where?"

He looked up at me. His eyes were very dark and very tired. "You've read the page. Seven loans. Six of you before you. Five of them didn't get far. The sixth did." He tapped the bottle against his knee. "I'm from his line. A world that made it to Year Nine."

Year Nine.

I'd died in Year Seven, on the hundredth floor, with the sky counting down to liquidation. I'd never seen Year Eight. I'd never known anyone who had.

"The Year Seven review," I said. "The liquidation."

"Passed." He said it plainly, like a weather report. "Not by much. *Acceptable,* the sky said. Nobody cheered, because nobody knew how close it had been. Except him." He almost smiled. "There was an Appraiser called Marsh who ran a trading house out of the old guild hall district. Fair prices, honest contracts, everything appraised in public. People called it the Marsh Exchange. As a joke, at first. Then not."

"I ran a trading house."

"He did." Silas glanced at me. "You don't look much like him. He was older. Tired around the eyes. He wrote everything in a ledger and he never let anyone carry it for him."

Nora's hand had gone still on the blanket.

"And me?" she said.

Silas turned his head to look at her. For a second, something moved across his face that I couldn't price.

"You were the Wall of Sixty," he said.

"The what?"

"Floor sixty. The Spire's sixtieth floor. Four guilds wiped on it in Year Five. Then you held it for eleven minutes alone while the Marsh Exchange expedition took the boss apart behind you, and after that, everyone called you that." He looked down at the bottle. "Best Warden in the world. Two hundred of you, give or take, and you were the one the others measured themselves against."

Nora didn't say anything for a while. Then she asked something I didn't expect.

"Was he okay? In that world. Did he ever eat anything good?"

Silas blinked at her. "Sundays," he said slowly. "There was a bakery next to the guild hall. I saw him come out of it with a paper bag a few times."

Nora looked at me. I looked at the window.

"Idiot," she said quietly. I didn't know which one of us she meant.

I was thinking about the door of the library, and the letters in the waterline, leaning hard to the right.

*DON'T LET HER HOLD THE LINE.*

"What happened in Year Nine?" I asked. "Day Three."

Silas didn't look up.

"I don't know," he said. "I'd left before that."

He said it easily. Too easily, maybe, or maybe I only thought so later. His right hand had moved to the hilt of the sword on his knees, the way some people touch their pocket when they say they've got their wallet.

I didn't push. I wrote it down in the back of my head, in the column where I keep the numbers that don't add up yet.

---

"So you borrowed," I said. "From him. The Lender."

"Same chair. Same pen. Same voice." Silas finally twisted the cap off the bottle and drank half of it in one go. "He sat down next to me in Year Nine and offered me one regression, back to Day Zero, at a rate I didn't argue with because I wasn't you. I had reasons to come back. Those are mine." He wiped his mouth with the back of his wrist. "Collateral was the standard alternate. Most valuable possession. I thought he meant my sword."

"He meant your guild."

"He meant my guild." Silas laughed without any sound. "I woke up on Day Zero in Zone Four, and by the end of the tutorial I had twenty-two people who'd signed with me, because I knew where the good loot was and I knew how not to die. And on the first night, a paper-colored window opened and told me that my collateral on file was the Cinder Pact Member Agreements. All twenty-two. Because a guild master's most valuable possession is his guild."

"How short were you?"

He looked at me for a moment.

"Less than you are," he said.

That was the worst thing he could have said, and he knew it, and he let it sit.

---

Bram turned around.

He didn't come over. He stood with his back to the window, and the window was dark, and the streetlamp outside made him into a black shape the size of a door.

"Why did you throw me out," he said.

It wasn't a question. Bram didn't seem to do questions.

Silas set the bottle on the floor. He took a while to answer, and when he did, he didn't look away.

"Because I knew the payment was going to fail by Day Five," he said. "And I knew what happens to collateral. I'd seen it in Year Nine." He paused. "Expelled members aren't on the roster. If you're not on the roster, you're not in the agreements. If you're not in the agreements, you can't be collected."

"The porter."

"I left him in the side room on purpose." Silas's voice was very even. "I knew you'd go back. You always go back. So I gave you a reason to break formation, and I took your armband for it in front of everyone, so nobody would ask why."

Nobody in the room moved.

Then Bram crossed it in three steps and hit him.

It wasn't a hard punch, for Bram. I'd seen him stop a Cinder Bailiff with his shoulder. This was an open-handed thing that came around from the side and caught Silas across the cheekbone and knocked his head back against the radiator with a dull iron *bong*. Jonah half stood up. Owen made a small noise. Silas didn't put up his hands. He just took it, and stayed where he was, and after a second he touched his cheek and looked at his fingers.

"That's fair," he said.

"The kid didn't know me," said Bram.

"I know."

"I carried him. He complained the whole way."

"I know, Bram."

Bram stood over him for a long time, breathing. Then he did something I didn't expect. He turned around and sat down on the floor next to Silas, with his back against the same radiator, his huge shoulders pressed against the smaller man's. He put his elbows on his knees and looked at the carpet.

Neither of them said anything else. They didn't need to.

Owen got up, took a second bottle of water out of the minibar, and put it in Bram's hand. Then he picked up his clipboard and wrote something. I read it later, upside down. *Day 7, night. Bram to Silas: one (1). Settled?*

The question mark was very Owen.

---

"I need you to buy them," Silas said.

He said it to me, quietly, after Owen had gotten him to let the burn on his neck be healed, and the room had gone back to something like ordinary.

"The twenty-two," he said. "Ashgrave buys distressed paper and collects on it, and once the collection's done, the agreements are just assets on a balance sheet. Assets can be sold. You read covenants. You bought Vanguard's charter off the board. You can buy them back."

I'd known it was coming. I'd known since the plaza, since he'd said *Marsh* and sat down at my feet. Nobody tells you a story like that for free.

I did the math anyway. Out loud, because I always do, and because I wanted Nora to hear it.

"Ashgrave bought the note at a discount," I said. "Even if they mark it down again, they'll ask at least a hundred a head. Twenty-two people. Twenty-two hundred Merit." I held up the ledger. "I have eighteen thirty-two. My second installment is two thousand, at noon on Day Thirteen, in six days. If I spend twenty-two hundred on your guild, I don't have twenty-two hundred, and I don't have two thousand, and on Day Thirteen, Nora's the one standing in a line on the plaza."

"Eli," said Nora.

"No," I said to Silas. "I'm sorry. No."

He nodded. He didn't argue. I think he'd known the answer before he asked, and had asked anyway, because asking was the only thing he had left to spend.

Nora was looking at me.

She didn't say anything else. She just looked, for a long time, the way she'd looked at the ledger page on the library steps. Not angry. Reading. I could feel her reading me, line by line, the way I read contracts, looking for the clause I hadn't said out loud.

I didn't know what she found. I didn't ask.

---

"Then make money," Silas said. "Fast. You need the first clear."

"I know."

"Do you know what it pays?"

"Twenty-four hundred, last time." I'd priced the gear of the people who didn't come back from it, in Week Three of the old life, from the back of a water cart. "Regional bonus, split by contribution. If we take it, my share's enough to cover Day Thirteen with room left over."

"Nobody's seen the boss room," Bram said, from the floor. "The Pact got to the seventh alcove this morning. That's the deepest anybody's been."

"Then we go deeper."

"There's one more thing." Silas leaned his head back against the radiator and closed his eyes. "Behind the ninth alcove. The back wall. There's a place where the stone's scorched in lines, like a ledger. Ruled columns. The sixth Marsh wrote something there."

The room went very quiet.

*Watch the walls in there,* Vesper had said, on the plaza, the day before. *The Kiln keeps books too.*

"When did he tell you that?" I said.

Silas didn't open his eyes. "A long time ago."

"What did he write?"

"I never went to look." His hand had found the sword hilt again. "It wasn't for me."

---

He left before midnight. Bram walked him to the door, and stood in the hall watching until he'd gone down the stairs, and then came back in and lay down on the floor next to Owen's sleeping bag with his shield for a pillow and said, to nobody, "He used to be better at lying."

Day Eight was arithmetic.

I spent the morning at the board with Owen, pricing every fire-resistance item in the region and buying none of them, because I had eighteen thirty-two Merit and couldn't spend any of it. I spent the afternoon at the hotel desk with a pencil and the back of a menu, drawing the Kiln corridor from memory. Alcove one through six, the ones we'd walked. Alcove seven, where the Pact had stopped. Eight and nine, blank. Six hundred seconds a cycle. Thirty seconds of warning. Five of fire. Bram's Ember Ward, twenty seconds, fifty-five percent, five minutes to recharge. Two cycles per ward, with time to spare.

Jonah came by and looked at the drawing for a while and said, "You can't draw."

"I can count."

"That's not the same thing," said Jonah, and went back to waxing his new bowstring.

Nora spent Day Eight with Bram behind the hotel, in the loading bay, hitting a sandbag he'd filled with gravel. I watched them from the window for a while. She was good. She'd been good since the leeches. She stood in front of the bag the way you stand in front of a door you're not going to let anyone through, and every time Bram swung the bag at her, she met it with the flat of her axe and didn't give an inch.

Every time, I thought about a line on the plaza. A gray cord. A polite stranger.

*Wall of Sixty.*

At dinner she came up the stairs with gravel dust on her knuckles and sat down on the end of my bed.

"You said no fast," she said. "Last night."

"I did the math."

"I know. I watched you do it." She picked at the tape on her knuckles. "Two thousand on Day Thirteen. Twenty-two hundred for them. You can't do both. I get it. I'm not stupid."

"Then what?"

"Nothing. I just wanted to see what your face does when you say no to somebody who's begging." She pulled a strip of tape off and rolled it into a ball between her fingers. "It doesn't do anything. That's what bothered me. It looked like you were pricing a sword."

"That's not fair."

"I know it's not." She flicked the tape ball into the trash can by the desk. It went in. "That's why I'm saying it out loud, instead of thinking it at you for a week." She stood up. "Eat something. You'll count better."

She went to shower. I sat there and looked at the ledger, and the number at the bottom of it, which was still eighteen thirty-two, and which didn't care what my face did.

---

She was asleep by eleven. I wasn't.

I sat on the other bed with the lamp off and the ledger on my knees and listened to her breathe, the way I had on the floor of the lounge in Harlan Hall, on the first night. Owen was asleep by the door. Bram was asleep on the floor, snoring. Jonah was in the bathtub again.

I hadn't appraised her since the dorm.

I'd told myself it didn't matter. That the lines wouldn't change. *Pledged as Collateral. Lienholder (Current): Private Lender. Lienholder (Prior): E█i M█rsh.* I knew them by heart. Reading them again would only make them true again.

But Silas had said *Year Nine* in a hotel room, out loud, like a place you could go. And Vesper had said *a door that opens from the other side.* And there was a wall in the Kiln with my handwriting on it, maybe, that someone had told a stranger about a long time ago, in a world that didn't exist anymore.

I looked at my sister in the dark.

Then, under the last one, in a color darker than the rest, a line I had never seen before.
