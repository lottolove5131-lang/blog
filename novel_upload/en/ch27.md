# Chapter 27: Noon

We left the Merchant Row Hotel at 06:00 on Day Thirty, thirty-six people and a handcart, and the first bounty hunters were waiting at the end of the street.

There were five of them. Two Warriors, a Rogue, a Ranger, and a man with a fishing net over his shoulder who, as far as I could tell, had no class at all, only optimism. They stood in a line across Merchant Row in the gray light, and the one in the middle held up his hand like a crossing guard.

"We just want the girl," he said. "Nobody has to—"

Bram walked into him.

Not fast. Bram never did anything fast. He just kept walking with his shield in front of him, and the shield was taller than the man, and the man discovered that he had to go somewhere and the only somewhere available was backward. His friends went with him. The man with the net sat down on the curb.

"Good morning," said Owen politely, stepping around him with his clipboard.

**[Day 30 — 06:04:12]**

Five hours, fifty-five minutes and forty-eight seconds.

I'd set the clock in the corner of my vision at midnight. I didn't take it down.

---

The river road ran south out of the city along the old channel, between warehouses and a long concrete flood wall. We walked it in a column. Bram and Nora at the front, shields side by side, edges touching. The fifteen from Vanguard in two files behind them. Jonah on the flank with his bow strung. Owen in the middle with the customers, keeping them together the way he'd kept eleven students together in a dorm lounge on the first night. Silas at the back, alone, because that was where he liked to be. Maya's handcart rattling behind him, full of water and bandages and spare straps.

Me in the center. Where they'd put me.

"You're the package," Nora had said, at five that morning, while she buckled her shield on. "I'm the bounty, but you're the package. If you don't get to the door, nothing else matters. So you walk in the middle and you don't be brave."

"I'm never brave."

"You jumped on a paper monster."

"Once."

By eight we'd been stopped four more times. Little groups, two or three or six, who'd seen the board at midnight and done the same math. A thousand Merit. More than most of them made in a month. They'd come out in the dark with ropes and nets and borrowed swords, and every time, they saw thirty-six people and two shields coming down the road, and most of them did a second piece of math and went home.

Not all of them.

At the old rail bridge over the channel, there were forty.

---

They'd set up properly. Somebody had organized it. Two wagons pulled across the far end of the bridge, and people standing on the wagons and in front of them, and more on the embankment on both sides. Some of them had guild armbands I didn't recognize. Some had nothing. A few had the look of people who'd been out all night and had nothing to lose by staying out a little longer.

**[Day 30 — 08:41:55]**

"We go around," said Silas quietly, at my shoulder.

"The next bridge is six kilometers east. That's two hours."

"Then we go through."

"Forty people," I said. "Who haven't done anything except read a notice."

I was still working it out when the column started moving without me.

Not the front. The middle. Owen's group. The customers.

The student went first. The kid who'd almost sold a leech core for three Merit on the stall's first day. He walked right past Bram's shield, out onto the open bridge, and stopped about ten meters from the wagons. He was maybe nineteen, and his voice cracked when he raised it.

"Hey! You! With the hand!"

One of the men on the wagons, a big Warrior with a bandaged hand, squinted.

"Is that—what are you doing here, kid?"

"What are *you* doing?" The kid pointed back at me. "That's the clipboard guy! He told me my core was worth six! He told you about the clause in your party agreement! You *bought me a drink* because of him!"

The Warrior on the wagon opened his mouth and didn't say anything.

Then the Artisan in the hardware-store apron walked out next to the kid, with his arms folded. "He read my contract for free," he said, loud enough for the embankment to hear. "Three of them. He found a clause that would've taken half my sales for a year. He charged me two percent of nothing." He nodded at the wagons. "I know at least six of you. You sold me weeds on Day Five."

The two sisters went next. Then the others, one by one, until there were fourteen people from the stall standing on the bridge in front of our shields with no weapons at all, and on the wagons, forty bounty hunters were looking at their neighbors.

"This is stupid," somebody on the embankment said. "It's a thousand Merit."

"Split forty ways," said Owen, from behind his clipboard. He'd done the math. Of course he had. "That's twenty-five each. Before the Exchange takes five percent."

There was a long silence on the bridge.

Then the Warrior with the bandaged hand climbed down off his wagon, put his shoulder against it, and started pushing it out of the way.

**[Day 30 — 09:02:17]**

We'd lost twenty minutes. I didn't care. I walked past the kid on the bridge and he grinned at me, and I couldn't think of anything to say, so I said, "Thank you," and he looked like I'd given him something worth a lot more than six Merit.

---

Zone Nine had been a lake on Day Zero. On Day Thirty, it was a field of dried mud as wide as a city, cracked into plates the size of tables, and it smelled of rot and river.

We came over the levee at 11:08, an hour and eight minutes later than I'd planned, and stopped for a second at the top to look.

The old oak trees were still standing out in the middle, gray and leafless. Harlan Hall was on the left, with the waterline still drawn across its brick at the height of the second floor, and somebody's laundry hanging out of a fourth-floor window. Further out, half sunk in the mud, the Hollow Matron's ribs stuck up like the frame of a boat nobody had finished building.

And past all of it, small and brick and lit from inside with the same cold light I remembered from the first night, Hale Memorial Library.

There were people on its steps.

Twenty-two of them. They stood across the steps in three ranks, all the way up to the landing, gray cords on their wrists. They didn't talk to each other. They didn't shift their weight. They stood like furniture that had been delivered and not yet unpacked.

At the bottom of the steps, in front of them, a man in good armor was waiting with a sword across his shoulder.

He had a gray cord on his wrist too.

**[Appraise]**

**[Gray Cord (Ashgrave) — Worn voluntarily.]**
**[Contract: Field Agent of record. Fee: per completed collection.]**

He'd tied it himself.

**[Day 30 — 11:09:40]**

"Fifty minutes," I said.

"Then walk," said Nora, and we went down into the mud.

I did the math out loud as we went. "One point two kilometers from the levee to the steps. Three kilometers an hour in this. Twenty-four minutes. We get there around 11:33. That leaves twenty-seven."

"Twenty-seven minutes to get through twenty-two people," said Owen.

"And Damon."

"And Damon."

I looked around while I walked. On the field, on the steps, in the shadow of the ribs. No paper-colored chair anywhere. The Lender had said he'd be at the door, in his professional capacity.

Not seeing him was worse.

---

It took us twenty-six of those minutes to cross. The mud plates shifted under our weight, and between them the old ground was soft enough to take a boot to the ankle. Maya's handcart sank to the axle twice, and twice the fifteen lifted it out without being asked. We passed the Matron's ribs close enough to touch. Somebody had carved initials into one of them. Twice, bounty hunters came at us from the ruins of the boathouse, small groups who'd gotten here first and hidden. The fifteen from Vanguard dealt with them. Jonah put an arrow through a net in midair, which I would not have believed if I hadn't seen it.

The first time a group came at the column's side, Nora stopped walking. I saw her do it. Her chin came up and her shoulders squared and her mouth opened, and I knew the words before she said them, because I'd heard them in the dorm and in the library and in the Counting House.

Then Bram's shield touched the edge of hers. Just touched.

She shut her mouth. She stayed where she was.

**[Day 30 — 11:35:02]**

We reached the bottom of the steps.

Damon smiled at me. It was the same smile from the courthouse on Day Zero. The same smile from the hundredth floor. It had never moved much, and it didn't move now.

"Eli," he said. "You're late."

"I had stops."

"I'm being paid to keep you off those steps until noon. Ashgrave doesn't mind how. I'd honestly rather just talk." He tipped his sword toward the collectors behind him. "They'll stand there until I tell them otherwise. You know these people, Okafor. You won't hit them. So it's a lot of shoving, for a long time, and then it's noon."

Bram's jaw worked. He didn't say anything.

"Twenty-three minutes," said Damon. "Do you want to spend them shoving?"

Silas walked past me.

He hadn't said a word all morning. He walked down the front of our column, past Bram and past Nora, with his dueling blade held low and loose, and stopped three meters in front of Damon.

"In Year Nine," he said, "I had your job."

Damon's smile stayed where it was. "I've heard."

"Field agent for Ashgrave. Gray cord. Good pay. I walked a man to a chair and told myself it was a kindness." Silas lifted the blade an inch. "Do you want me to tell you how it ends?"

"Not really."

"I'll show you, then."

He moved.

I've seen a lot of fights. I've never seen anything like what Silas Rook did with a dueling blade in the next ten seconds. Damon was a Warrior, level fifteen, with better armor and a longer sword and forty kilos on him. It didn't matter. Silas went inside his first swing and turned the second one off his blade like water off glass and put his shoulder into Damon's chest, and the two of them went down together into the mud at the foot of the steps, and Silas came up on top with his knee on Damon's sword arm.

And Bram stepped forward and planted his shield in the mud between them and the collectors.

Line of sight.

The twenty-two stopped. All of them, at once. Not still, exactly. Waiting. Like clerks whose next form hadn't arrived.

"*Now,*" said Bram.

---

**[Day 30 — 11:44:51]**

It wasn't clean. Damon was shouting from under Silas's knee, and every time his head came up past the edge of Bram's shield, the collectors twitched toward us again, and Bram had to shift the shield, and shift it again.

Bram talked while he did it. Not to us. He was looking at the collectors one after the other and saying their names, first names, nicknames, the name of somebody's dog. "It's me," he said. "It's Bram." Nobody answered. He kept saying the names. "It's Bram. I came back for the porter, remember? You said I was an idiot."

We went up the right side of the steps, single file, in the gap the shield made. The fifteen from Vanguard came behind us and stood in front of the collectors with their arms out, not touching, just there. Owen stood at the bottom with his hands over two of the customers who'd been cut on the bridge, healing them, talking to them, keeping them calm.

Halfway up, one of the collectors at the end of the line turned. Her head came round past the edge of Bram's shield and she saw Damon in the mud, and she saw the line of us, and she came for me with her hands out and nothing at all in her eyes.

Nora was beside me.

I saw it again. The chin. The shoulders. The breath.

She didn't say it.

She stepped in front of me with her shield, and Bram, from twelve steps down, roared her name, and threw his whole body sideways along the steps, and his shield slammed in beside hers with a crash I felt in my teeth. Edges together. Two shields. The collector hit both of them and stopped, and stood there with her palms flat against the gray paint, and didn't push.

"I didn't," Nora said. She was breathing hard. "I didn't, Eli, I didn't use it—"

"I know."

"I *wanted* to—"

"I know. Go."

**[Day 30 — 11:58:30]**

She went one way along the landing and Bram went the other, holding the steps between them. I went up the middle.

The last three steps. The landing. The pillars. The two tall wooden doors.

**[Day 30 — 11:59:50]**

---

I stopped in front of the doors and put my hand flat against the wood.

The waterline stain still ran across both of them at chest height, pale and old, the way it had on the first night. The letters were still cut into it. Uneven. Cramped. Leaning hard to the right, with the *t*s crossed too high.

*DON'T LET HER HOLD THE LINE.*
*— E.*

Next to the left-hand door, there was a tall arched window. The glass was dusty. Through it, I could see the reading room. The long tables. The green lamps, turned warm and yellow since the day the Librarian died.

And at the circulation desk in the middle of the room, where the paper monster had once stood up to meet us, Vesper Kaine was sitting in a wooden chair with her gray coat over her shoulders and a thick file open on the desk in front of her.

I didn't need to appraise it. I could read the tab from the window.

*Loan #6 — Marsh, N.*

She looked up. She saw me through the glass. She didn't smile, and she didn't look away.

**[Day 30 — 11:59:57]**

Under my palm, the wood got warm.

The two letters at the bottom of the carving had started to glow. Faint at first. Then brighter. Not the Exchange's gold, and not the Lender's paper. A dark, wet red, the color of ruled lines on an old ledger page, filling the scratches from the inside like ink soaking up through a blotter.

*— E.*

From the other side of the door, someone knocked.

Two quick.

A pause.
