# Chapter 15: Clipboard House

I appraised Damon before I'd decided to.

He was still standing beside Vesper at the gray board, smiling at me across forty meters of plaza, and my skill went out and read him the way it reads anything I look at too long.

**[Damon Kreel — Human — Warrior (Lv. 13)]**
**[Engagement: Ashgrave Recoveries — Field Agent (Region 14).]**
**[Duties: Reporting on assigned collateral. Asset: Nora Marsh.]**
**[Fee: 40 Merit per report. Reports filed: 2.]**
**[Market Value: 11 Merit]**

Two reports. The notice said effective Day Thirteen, and it was the evening of Day Thirteen, and he'd already filed two.

"What does it say?" Nora asked.

I told her. I tried to say it the way I'd say a price, flat. It didn't come out flat.

"Reports," she said. "On me."

"Where you sleep. When you eat. Who you stand next to." I looked at the plaza, at the place where Bram had been planting his shield all afternoon while four kids threw bottles at him and my sister. Damon had been in the plaza at noon. I'd seen his back near the noodle stall and thought nothing of it. "Probably what you were practicing today."

"He's selling my *breakfast schedule*."

"He's selling everything he knows about you to the people who collect you on Day Thirty. At forty a time." I did the math because I couldn't stop myself. "A report a day for seventeen days is six hundred eighty. Plus a bonus on delivery, if I know how these firms work. He'll clear his interest to me out of your habits."

Nora's hand had gone tight on the strap of her axe.

Across the plaza, Damon lifted two fingers to his forehead in a small salute. Then he turned and followed Vesper into the Ashgrave office, and the door shut behind them.

I wanted to walk across the plaza. I had a picture of it in my head, very clear: the door, his face, my hand. I'm not a fighter. I've never been a fighter. I'd have lost, and it would have felt wonderful for about four seconds.

"Eli," Nora said.

"I know."

"You've got that face."

"I know." I made my hands open. "He's a Warrior at thirteen and I'm an Appraiser at twelve, and I owe a man three thousand five hundred in ten days. He's not the number that matters this week."

"He's the number that's *watching me*."

"Then let him watch you get stronger," I said. "He has to write that down too."

---

Owen had the idea. I want that on the record, since he'll never say it himself.

We were in the hotel lobby that night, the five of us around the desk that wasn't a breakfast table, and Nora had the ledger open between us with her two columns. *Need: 2,705. Days: 10.* Jonah suggested going back into the Kiln to farm the corridor constructs. Bram said the corridor had already been picked clean by the twenty parties that went in after our notice. I said the second floor wouldn't open until Day Seventeen.

"Can I say something?" Owen said.

We looked at him. He'd been quiet all evening, drawing small boxes in the margin of his clipboard.

"People keep coming up to you," he said to me. "All day. Since the courthouse steps. They hold out a contract and ask you to read it. A girl from Zone Eleven asked you about a sword. That man asked you if his core was real. You read them all for free."

"I said I would. On the steps."

"I know. That was good." He tapped the clipboard. "But you're giving away the one thing you have that nobody else has, and you have to find two thousand seven hundred Merit by Day Twenty-Three. I counted. Today you did nineteen appraisals for other people. If you charged two percent of what you told them the thing was worth—"

"I'm not charging people to read their own contracts."

"Two percent," Owen said. "The board takes five. You'd be the cheapest honest price in the city." He turned the clipboard around. He'd written it already, in his careful RA block capitals, the kind you see on a laundry room sign.

*CLIPBOARD HOUSE*
*Contracts read. Loot appraised. Fair numbers.*
*Fee: 2%. Everything written down.*

"Clipboard House," Jonah said. "Seriously."

"It's his clipboard," Nora said. "It's a good name."

"I'll write every trade," Owen said. "Every one. What came in, what you said it was worth, what they paid. If anyone ever says we cheated them, I'll show them the page."

I looked at the sign for a long time. Then I looked at Nora's two columns.

"Two percent," I said. "Split with you. Half and half."

"Sixty-forty," Owen said. "It's your eyes."

"Half and half. It's your clipboard."

He wrote *50/50* at the bottom of the sign, and underlined it twice.

---

The courthouse square on Day Fourteen was a market.

Eight days ago it had been the place where Damon gave speeches. Now it was rows of blankets and folding tables and shopping carts, people selling monster parts and canned food and rewired phone chargers that ran off mana cores. The stall spots by the courthouse steps cost three Merit a day, paid to a big man in a reflective vest who'd decided he was in charge of the square and whom nobody had yet argued with.

We paid him three. Owen set up a folding table and two plastic chairs and taped the sign to the front of the table with the last of a roll of duct tape. I sat down. He sat down next to me with his clipboard and a pen and a spare pen.

The first hour, nobody came.

The second hour, a girl of about eighteen came, holding a guild contract in both hands. Behind her, two boys from the same zone, holding theirs.

"You're the one from the steps," she said. "With Vanguard."

"I'm the one from the steps."

"Can you read this? The guild's from Zone Eleven. Signing bonus is three hundred. They said it's standard."

**[Appraise]**

**[Guild Member Agreement (Zone 11) — Draft]**
**[Term: Six (6) months.]**
**[Signing Bonus: 300 Merit. Payable on signing.]**
**[Loot: Guild retains 60% of all drops for members below Lv. 10.]**
**[Hidden Clause: None.]**

"It's honest," I said. "Bad, but honest. They'll take sixty percent of everything you find until you're level ten. That's a lot. But it says so on page two, and they pay the bonus up front, and there's nothing hiding." I turned the window so she could read it. "It's a fair deal for a bad price. Your call."

She stared at the window. "That's it?"

"That's it."

She paid six Merit. Two percent of three hundred. Owen wrote it down: *Day 14. Contract, guild (Z11). Valued 300. Fee 6. Signed: no hidden.* The two boys behind her paid six each, and one of them, after he'd read his, folded it up and put it in his pocket and said he'd think about it.

At noon a skinny kid in a university hoodie came to the table with a Silt Leech core in his palm. He looked like he'd been carrying it around for days.

"There's a guy at the end of the row who'll give me three for it," he said. "Is three good?"

**[Silt Leech Core — Market Value: 6 Merit | True Value: 8 Merit]**

"It's half," I said. "Six on the board. Go list it yourself, you'll pay a five percent fee and still clear five."

"I didn't know you could list on the board yourself."

"Everybody can. Nobody tells you."

He paid nothing, because two percent of six rounded down to zero, and Owen wrote it down anyway. *Core, leech. Valued 6. Fee 0. Advised: list.* Then he wrote a small arrow beside *a guy at the end of the row*, and looked down the row.

In the afternoon, Maya came.

I hadn't seen her since the Drowned Campus. Her braid was shorter, singed at the end, and she was carrying a crate. In the crate were twelve leather shield straps, padded and riveted, with a strip of something gray and fibrous stitched inside the padding.

"I'm selling them for fifteen each," she said. "Nobody's buying. Can you tell me if they're any good? Honestly. Not nice."

**[Reinforced Shield Strap (Artisan, Maya Reyes) — Market Value: 15 Merit | True Value: 70 Merit]**
**[Lining: Kiln construct fiber. Heat transfer to wearer −30%.]**

I read it twice.

"Maya," I said. "You put construct fiber in the lining."

"From the scrap. It was cheap."

"It cuts heat through the shield by thirty percent." I turned the window to her. "Bram held a shield in a furnace for five minutes last week and Owen had to heal his hands every thirty seconds. These are worth seventy. You're selling them for fifteen because nobody can see what you did."

She stared at the window for a long time. Then she said, quietly, "Seventy?"

"Ask fifty. Show people this window. They'll pay fifty."

Owen wrote it all down. Then he leaned past me and said, "Can we buy two at fifty? For Bram and Nora."

"For you," Maya said, "fifteen."

"Fifty," Owen said. "It's written down now."

---

The guy at the end of the row didn't give a name.

He had a cart with a hand-painted sign on it that said FAIR PRICE BUYBACK — CERTIFIED APPRAISER, and a little brass monocle on a chain, and a line of about ten people waiting with things in their hands. Most of them were new arrivals from the outer zones. You could tell by the mud.

I watched him for half an hour while Owen took a contract from a Cleric. He took each item, put his monocle to his eye, frowned at it, and named a price. The price was always low. The people always took it, because he was a certified appraiser and they were tired and didn't know what anything was worth.

At three o'clock, a woman with a toddler on her hip held out a Kiln-Heart Core. She must have found it in the corridor, in the scrap the parties left behind. The buyback man put up his monocle.

"Cracked," he said. "Forty."

**[Kiln-Heart Core — Market Value: 150 Merit | True Value: 600 Merit]**
**[Condition: Intact.]**

I got up.

Owen said, "Eli," in the voice he uses when he's about to have to write something down that he'd rather not.

I walked down the row. I didn't hurry. I'd done this once on the courthouse steps and I'd learned that you don't need to hurry. You just need to be holding the right window.

"Excuse me," I said to the woman. "Before you sell. Can I show you something?"

The buyback man looked at me. He knew who I was. Everybody in the square did by then.

"Ma'am, this is a private trade," he said.

"It's a public square." I held out my hand, palm up, and opened the appraisal where everyone in line could read it. "Kiln-Heart Core. Market value one fifty. Intact. Not cracked." I looked at him. "I sold one of these on Day Six. I know what they weigh."

The line went quiet.

"He's a certified—" someone started.

"Certified by who?" I said. And because I'm an Appraiser, and because he was standing three feet away from me and had been standing there all afternoon, I'd already read him.

**[Unregistered trader — Human — Rogue (Lv. 7)]**
**[Skills: Sleight of Hand, Fast Talk.]**
**[Brass Monocle — Market Value: 1 Merit. Function: None.]**

I didn't say it out loud. I just turned the window so the line could read it.

A man near the back said, "Rogue," the way you'd say *rat*.

He looked at the window. Then at the line. Then he put his monocle in his pocket, very carefully, as if it were worth more than one Merit, and started pushing his cart away down the row. Nobody stopped him. Nobody had to. Half the line followed me back to the folding table.

The woman with the toddler listed her core on the board herself, with Owen standing beside her reading out the menu. It sold in eleven minutes for one hundred forty-two after the fee.

She came back to the table and tried to give me twenty.

"Two percent," Owen said firmly. "Three Merit."

"Three," she said. "That's insane."

"It's written down," Owen said, and wrote it down.

---

On the afternoon of Day Fifteen, Damon walked past the stall.

He didn't join the line. He stopped three meters from the table with a small notebook in his hand, looked over the queue, looked at Bram behind me, and then looked across the square to where Nora was practicing with Bram's spare shield. He wrote something down.

"Business is good," he said. "I'll put that in. The firm will like it. The collateral's brother found a way to earn."

"Forty a report, wasn't it?" I said. "Write that one for free. My treat."

He smiled, closed the notebook, and walked on. Bram didn't take his hand off his shield until Damon's back was gone in the crowd.

Owen wrote that down too. *Day 15. D. Kreel, visit. No trade.*

---

By the end of Day Fifteen, the line was forty people long at noon and didn't finish until after dark. Jonah came to stand at the end of it so that nobody cut in. Bram came to stand behind the table, because, he said, honest businesses attract dishonest visitors, and a two-and-a-half-meter Bulwark is cheaper than a lock.

Owen filled eleven pages of his clipboard in two days. Then he ran out of paper and started writing on the backs of the Zone Eleven girl's spare contract pages, which she'd given him for that purpose.

At closing on Day Fifteen he added it up.

"Eleven Merit for you on Day Fourteen," he said. "Twenty-three today. After the stall fee."

"Thirty-four."

"Thirty-four."

"In two days. We need two thousand seven hundred."

"It's not about the thirty-four," Owen said. He held up the clipboard. "It's about the forty people. Next week it's a hundred. And on Day Twenty-Three, when you need it, you'll have a hundred people in this city who know your numbers are real." He paused. "That's worth something. I don't know what. You're the one who can see it."

I didn't try to appraise it. I think I was afraid I'd get *Unable to appraise*.

---

That night, I did the thing I'd started doing every night since the Kiln. I waited until Nora was asleep, on the other bed this time, back in our own room, with her boots still on and her new shield strap clutched in one hand like a stuffed animal. Then I appraised her.

I knew all the lines by now. Warden, Level Thirteen. Not for sale. Pledged as collateral. The Lender. The senior lien, with its name scratched out and its date in Year Nine.

And under the lien, the line in old ink that had pointed me to the ninth alcove of the Kiln. I'd stopped expecting it to say anything new.

It had changed now.

The old paper color had gone darker, almost brown, like a page someone had held too close to a candle.

**[Door: Hale Memorial Library.]**
**[Opens: Day 30, 12:00:00.]**

I knew that door. I'd stood in front of it in the tutorial, one step off a rowboat, with the black lake lapping at the third step behind me. I'd read the letters someone had scratched into the waterline stain with a knife, leaning hard to the right, the *t*s crossed too high.

*DON'T LET HER HOLD THE LINE.*

The door that opened from the other side wasn't a figure of speech. It was in Zone Nine. It had been there the whole time.

And it would open at noon, on the same day the firm was coming to take her.
