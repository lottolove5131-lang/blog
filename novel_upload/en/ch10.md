# Chapter 10: Collections

I went to see Damon alone, before breakfast, because I didn't want anyone watching my face while I did it.

Nora was still asleep when I left the hotel on Fifth Street. She'd gone to bed with her boots on and her axe propped against the nightstand, the way she'd slept every night since the dorm. Owen was on the floor by the door in a sleeping bag he'd found somewhere. Bram was snoring through the wall from the room next door like a slow engine.

I wrote *Back by ten* on the back of an Emberleaf receipt and left it on Nora's boot.

My left forearm itched under the bandage. Owen had healed most of the burn the night before, and what he couldn't heal he'd wrapped.

The Lender's last words had been sitting in my chest all night like a swallowed coin.

*One of them is in this Spire right now. First installment due tomorrow.*

Tomorrow was today.

---

The Merchant Row Hotel had been Vanguard's guild hall for three days. On the evening of Day Five, when I'd come looking for Nora, the lobby had been full of gray armbands and new armor and the noise of thirty people who thought they'd picked the winning side.

This morning it was a lobby.

A front desk with a brass bell on it. A potted fern that nobody had watered since the world ended. A long stretch of carpet with pale rectangles where the furniture had been, because somebody had already sold the furniture. A boy of maybe eighteen was asleep behind the desk with his cheek on the guest register, and his gray armband had slid down to his elbow.

**[VANGUARD — Members: 15]**

I didn't wake him.

I found Damon in the breakfast room. He was sitting alone at a table by the window with a cup of something that had stopped steaming a while ago, and his status window was open in front of him, turned so that I could see it from the doorway. I think he'd meant me to. Damon never did anything in a room without knowing where the door was.

He was wearing the rest of the armor. The breastplate, the right pauldron, the vambraces. Without the left shoulder piece he looked lopsided, like a statue somebody had started taking apart for scrap and then lost interest in.

"Eli," he said. "You're up early."

"Seventy and ninety-five," I said. "A hundred and sixty-five. If both sell at list, after the five percent fee, you clear about a hundred and fifty-six." I pulled out the chair across from him and sat down, uninvited. "You're twenty-four short."

"I've got a week."

"You've got five days." I held up five fingers, in case he wanted to count them. "A hundred and eighty, on Day Twelve. I'll collect it at noon."

He smiled.

That was what I'd come to see, I think. On the courthouse steps I'd watched it move at the corners, and some petty part of me had wanted to know if it had peeled the rest of the way overnight.

It hadn't. It was the same smile from the hundredth floor. Warm, even, a little sorry for you. If you didn't know him, you would have bought something from him.

"You'll get it," he said.

"I know I will."

**[Damon Kreel — Human — Warrior (Lv. 13)]**

Eleven.

On Day Zero, on the courthouse steps, the board had priced him at fourteen. Four days of leading a guild and two levels later, the market had marked him down by three. The true value hadn't moved at all. Nine, then. Nine now.

He saw me read it. He couldn't see the numbers, but he knew the look.

"Go on," he said. "What am I worth today?"

"Less," I said.

"Than fourteen?"

"Eleven."

He laughed. It was a real laugh. "You know what they're calling you in the square? *The man who reads fine print.* People are lining up at the board with their contracts, asking if anyone's seen you." He turned his cup a quarter turn on its saucer. "Fifteen people, Eli. That's what you left me. You know what fifteen people cost to feed?"

"About a hundred and eighty a week less than they cost you, if you'd paid them fairly in the first place."

"Your sister—"

I looked at him.

He stopped. He actually stopped, mid-word, and I watched him put the rest of the sentence back where it had come from. Then he smiled again, wider.

"I didn't say her name," he said softly.

"No."

"I'm keeping my end. You'll notice that." He picked up the cold cup and drank from it as if it were still hot. "Day Twelve. Noon. Enjoy your breakfast."

I got up. At the door I stopped, because I'd learned a long time ago that the last thing you say in a negotiation is the thing they remember.

"If I don't see it by twelve-oh-one," I said, "I call the note. And the fifteen people asleep upstairs find out who owns their guild."

"I know how a callable note works, Eli."

"You didn't on Day Four."

The boy at the desk was awake when I walked back through the lobby. He looked at me the way you look at a dog that has bitten someone you know. I nodded to him. He didn't nod back.

---

When I got back to Fifth Street, Jonah was restringing his bow on the edge of the bathtub.

I'd seen the bow in the Kiln, but in a burning corridor you only notice things afterward. Jonah was a Warrior. Day Zero, white room, rugby shirt, four seconds. He'd swung a sword at every leech on the fourth floor of Harlan Hall.

"Where did that come from?" I said.

"Plaza." He didn't look up. He was winding the new string around the lower nock with the same frown he used for everything. "Day Six, early. We came up out of Zone Nine at dawn and walked straight into the Iron Wolves going in. There was a porter at the door holding their spare gear. After the board said *Wiped*, he put it all down on the steps and walked away. Nobody touched it." He shrugged one big shoulder. "So I did."

"You're a Warrior."

"Sword's for leeches," Jonah said. "Bow's for things that breathe fire. I'm not getting close to anything that breathes fire."

That was the most I'd ever heard him say at once. Nora, sitting on the closed toilet lid eating dry cereal out of the box, raised her eyebrows at me.

"And yours?" I asked Owen, who was folding his sleeping bag into a square so neat it could have been sold.

He glanced at the shield leaning against the wall. It was clear polycarbonate, scuffed white along the edges, with CAMPUS SECURITY stenciled across the top in letters that were peeling at the corners.

"The security office in the student union," he said. "There were four of them in a locker. I took the one that wasn't cracked." He paused, then added, very seriously, "I wrote it down. On the clipboard. In case anyone comes back for it."

Of course he had.

---

I told them what the Lender had said at the edge of the square the night before. I told them what I wanted to do about it, which was to stand in the Spire plaza at noon and watch.

"Why?" said Nora.

"Because someone else in this city borrowed what I borrowed," I said. "And today's their first installment. And I'd like to know what happens to people who miss one."

She put the cereal box down.

Bram had come in by then, filling the doorway sideways. He'd heard the last part. He looked at the floor for a moment, then at me.

"The Cinder Pact went in at dawn," he said. "All of them. Twenty-three. Silas wanted the first clear before anyone else got close." He rubbed his thumb along the scorched rim of his shield. "Silas never goes anywhere without the whole guild."

"Who's Silas?"

"Silas Rook. Guild Master. Duelist." Bram's voice didn't change, but something underneath it did. "He's the one who took my armband."

---

The plaza at noon was busier than it had been on Day Six, and quieter.

The board by the message wall had posted the morning's notices. Two more parties wiped. One party out with a single survivor, sitting on the steps with a blanket around his shoulders. Someone was renting folding stools at two Merit an hour so people could sit and watch the black door.

I stood with my back to a lamppost and watched the clock in the corner of my vision.

11:58.

Nora stood next to me with her arms crossed. Bram stood behind us, and the crowd parted around him the way water parts around a pier. Jonah had his bow over his shoulder. Owen had his clipboard.

11:59.

12:00.

The sky didn't do anything. The window that opened was in front of my face, and only mine, and it was the color of old paper.

**[NOTICE OF SALE — Private Lender]**
**[Borrower: Silas Rook. First Installment: Unpaid. Status: Defaulted.]**
**[Note sold. Buyer: Ashgrave Recoveries.]**
**[Collateral: Cinder Pact Member Agreements (22).]**
**[Collection: Immediate.]**

I read it twice. The second time, I read it the way I'd read the loan on the hundredth floor, one word at a time, looking for the four words at the end that would change everything else.

*Member Agreements.*

He hadn't put up himself. He hadn't put up a sister. He'd put up his guild.

"Eli," Nora said. "What is it?"

I didn't answer. I was looking past her, at the black door, where a woman in a long gray coat had just walked out of the crowd.

Vesper Kaine didn't hurry. She didn't look at the board or the people on their rented stools. She walked across the plaza with her hands in her coat pockets, the way you walk to a bus stop when you know the bus will wait. As she passed us, she turned her head, just slightly, and met my eyes.

Then she walked through the black door into the Kiln, in the middle of a charge cycle, without slowing down.

Somebody on a stool said, "Is she *crazy*—"

The board updated.

---

It took seven minutes. I know, because I counted them, and I counted them because counting was the only thing my body would let me do.

At 12:07, the first one came out.

He was a young man in Cinder Pact red, carrying a sword he didn't seem to know he was holding. His armor was scorched on one side. He walked out of the black door at a normal pace, like a man leaving a bank, and stopped on the plaza stones, and stood there.

Then another came out behind him. And another.

They came out in single file. Nobody pushed and nobody spoke. They lined up on the plaza in a neat row, one beside the next, like people waiting to be served, and every one of them wore the same thing tied around the left wrist: a plain gray cord, knotted twice. Not an armband. Something thinner. Something that looked like the string you'd tie around a bundle of paper.

Their eyes were open. They looked at the plaza. They didn't look at anything in it.

The crowd had gone quiet. The bread seller had stopped with a loaf in each hand.

Behind me, Bram made a sound I'd never heard a person make.

He went past me before I could stop him. People scrambled out of his way. He went straight down the line, looking at faces, and I watched him find the one he was looking for: a skinny kid, maybe nineteen, at the very end, with a porter's frame still strapped to his back and a bandage around one knee.

"Hey," Bram said. "Hey. Kid."

The kid looked up at him. Politely. Like a stranger who'd been asked the time.

"It's me." Bram crouched so they were eye to eye, which put his head about level with the kid's. "Side room in Zone Four. You had the bad knee. I came back for you. I *carried* you, you complained about it the whole way—"

"I'm sorry," the kid said. His voice was flat and pleasant. "I don't think we've met."

Bram stayed crouched there.

He tried two more. A woman with a cleric's staff stepped half a pace away from him. A man with a scar through one eyebrow said, "Do you need help?" in a voice like a recorded message. Nobody knew him. Nobody knew anyone. They stood in their line with their gray cords and their open eyes, waiting for whatever came next.

I made myself do it. It was rude, and everybody does it, and I'm an Appraiser.

*Memory: Seized.*

It hadn't killed them. It had just taken them, quietly and in full, the way the Lender took things. Whatever they'd been yesterday, whatever they'd remembered, the knee and the side room and the man who'd carried them, all of it was in someone else's ledger now. What was left on the plaza was the collateral.

I looked at the kid's wrist. The gray cord.

I looked at Nora.

She was standing exactly where I'd left her, with her arms still crossed, watching Bram crouched in front of a boy who didn't know him. She had our mother's face. I only knew that because people told us.

*Collection on Loan Six is scheduled for Day Thirty.*

This was what it looked like. Vesper Kaine had walked across the plaza to show me, without saying a single word: a gray cord, and a line, and a polite stranger who used to be my sister asking Bram if he needed help. There was nothing in it I could appraise my way through.

Twenty-three days.

I had a hundred and sixty-eight Merit to find in six days, and after that, whatever the next statement said, and after that, it didn't matter how much I found. On Day Thirty, Vesper would walk up to her, and Nora would walk out of wherever she was, at a normal pace, and stand in a line.

"Eli." Nora was looking at me now instead of at Bram. "You've gone white again."

"I'm fine."

"You're lying."

"I'm counting," I said. "Both things can be true."

---

Vesper came out last.

Twenty-two people in a line, and then the gray coat, walking out of the heat as if it were weather. She went down the line once, touching nothing, checking each wrist with a glance, like a clerk checking a stack of forms for signatures. Then she lifted one hand, and the line turned, all twenty-two at once, and followed her across the plaza and down the avenue toward the river. The crowd let them through without a sound.

She didn't look at me again. She didn't need to.

One.

The board had it right. A few minutes later, the black door gave up one more person.

He came out bent over, with one hand on the doorframe, coughing. Mid-thirties. A long thin sword in a scabbard at his hip, the kind of blade that only makes sense if you plan on fighting one person at a time. Dark hair singed short on one side. His Cinder Pact red was black from the waist down, and he wasn't wearing a gray cord.

Of course he wasn't. The collateral had been the guild. The man who'd pledged it still belonged to himself.

He stood in the doorway and looked at the empty plaza where his guild had lined up. Then he walked. Not toward the avenue where Vesper had taken them. Not toward the bread sellers or the board. He walked toward us, badly, listing to one side, and Bram stood up from where he'd been crouching and watched him come, and didn't move, and didn't say a word.

He reached the lamppost I was leaning against and sat down on the plaza stones beside my feet, heavily, like a man sitting down at the end of a very long shift. He leaned his head back against the post. He closed his eyes.

Then he opened them and looked up at me, and he didn't look surprised to see me at all.

"Marsh," he said. His voice was scraped raw from the heat. "You're the seventh, aren't you?"

The plaza noise went somewhere far away.

"I knew the sixth," said Silas Rook.
